# LIV credential strategy: jurisdiction, own standards, language and regional focus

Status (October 9, 2026): recommendations for owner decision (D-S.1 to D-S.5 in `BUILD-PLAN.md` §5). Nothing here changes the site, a credential or a scheme. The HSE Governance scheme stays IN DEVELOPMENT and this memo does not define it.

Evidence: three verification passes on October 9, 2026 read the official texts of U.S. and UK frameworks and the published pages of credentialing bodies. Copies of the pages and PDFs are kept outside the repository for the reviewer. Sites that refused automated readers were not bypassed; points that no official copy confirmed are marked **not verified** below.

## Starting point (owner's position)
LIV is a U.S. professional credentialing body (LIV LLC, Virginia). It creates its own professional credentials on its own authority, following the international model, from a U.S. and international perspective. LIV states what it is and what it does. It does not claim what it does not hold, and it does not need defensive denials of things it never claimed.

Wording note: in English, "accreditation" usually means a recognition granted to an organization by an accreditation body. What LIV develops and awards is a **credential** (or certification, once a scheme reaches that class). Using "credential" keeps the message strong and exact.

## Q1. Should a LIV credential be built on Saudi or GCC law?
**Recommendation:** no, not as its base. Build the core credential on international standards and principles that apply in any country. Add the law of each country as a separate **regional application module** on top of the core.

Why:
1. **Portability is what gives a credential its strength.** Bodies whose credentials are held in many countries keep the core free of any one country's law:
   - IAPP: the CIPM and CIPT are global. Law sits in separate regional credentials (CIPP/E, CIPP/US, CIPP/A and others). The FIP designation rewards holding both kinds [C1].
   - IIA: the CIA is tied to the Global Internal Audit Standards, not to a national law [C7].
   - Global Competent Boards: themes only, no national law modules [C5].
2. **Bodies that do include law localize it per country:**
   - Chartered Governance Institute: one global title, but the Company Law module follows each division's country. UK & Ireland teaches the Companies Act 2006; Hong Kong teaches Hong Kong company law [C2].
   - ICA: international core qualifications, plus local certificates such as the UAE certificate built with the Ministry of Economy, open only to UAE practitioners [C4].
   - ACAMS: a global core, with optional regional electives [C6].
3. **A credential built on one country's law is narrow.** ISO/IEC 17024 certifies "for a specific scope" defined by a job or practice analysis [C8]. A Saudi-law base would limit the credential to roles that apply Saudi law, and every amendment to that law would force a scheme revision.
4. **The U.S. and UK frameworks show the same design.** Their most widely used governance and compliance frameworks are written as principles any organization can apply, and they scale to small organizations:
   - U.S. Sentencing Guidelines §8B2.1: seven minimum elements of an effective compliance and ethics program; small organizations may meet them with less formality [U7a].
   - DOJ Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs, updated September 2024: is the program well designed, resourced and empowered, and does it work in practice [U7b].
   - UK Bribery Act 2010 s.7 with the Ministry of Justice's six principles; ECCTA 2023 s.199 with the Home Office's six principles [K5, K6].
   - Binding rules such as Sarbanes-Oxley, Dodd-Frank and the listing standards apply only to listed issuers [U1, U2, U4]. The U.S. has no binding federal governance code for private or family companies [U9].

Proposed structure (names are placeholders until scheme governance approves them):

| Layer | Content | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Core | LIV Body of Knowledge: ISO 37000, 37001, 37301, 31000, 45001, the G20/OECD Principles, COSO; U.S. and UK frameworks as reference models | The credential itself; the same in every country |
| Reference frameworks | U.S. (LIV's home jurisdiction): §8B2.1, DOJ ECCP, SOX and S-K governance disclosures, Delaware board law, the OSH Act. UK: CA 2006 directors' duties, the UK Code 2024, Wates, the Bribery Act and ECCTA, HSWA | Comparative content in the core: how mature systems put the principles into law |
| Regional application | Saudi Arabia first, then the UAE and the other GCC states: Companies Law, CMA rules, Labor Law, OSH regulations (from the GCC map) | A separate module or endorsement on top of the core, written and reviewed by licensed lawyers in that country, re-checked every 6 months |

Guidance about a country's law (articles, handbooks, the GCC map) stays reference content and is published only under a licensed reviewer's name (K.8). Whether a local authority accepts a credential is decided locally; the country acceptance guides (M.3) state it from official sources.

## Q3. Can LIV publish its own standards?
**Recommendation:** yes. Publish LIV's own **body of knowledge** and **competence standard** first; they are what the credential examines. Organizational practice standards come later. Call them "LIV standards", issued under a written due process.

What makes a self-published standard credible, from bodies that do it [C9–C13]:
1. Written procedures, published.
2. A balanced committee, with no single interest holding a majority (NFPA: at most one third per interest; ASTM: producers and non-producers; PMI: no interest category in the majority).
3. A public comment period, with a written answer to every comment and objection.
4. Approval by a supermajority (PMI and ANSI: at least two thirds).
5. A procedural appeals route.
6. Dated editions and a fixed review cycle (5 years at ANSI, PMI and ASTM).
7. Independent oversight (the IIA's IPPF Oversight Council).

For a credential body, ISO/IEC 17024 adds:
- a job or practice analysis that identifies the tasks, the competence each task needs, the prerequisites, the assessment and the recertification;
- scheme development by appropriate experts, with no single interest predominating [C8].
SHRM built its body of knowledge from focus groups, a large survey and repeated practice analyses across regions, and keeps exam writers apart from study-material writers [C12].

Future option: ANSI accreditation as a standards developer, which is the precondition for an American National Standard [C14].
- It needs the procedures above, ANSI review and periodic audits.
- Until ANSI grants it, LIV standards are described only as "LIV standards".
- Which ANSI edition is current could not be confirmed (ansi.org refused automated readers); the summary used the January 2022 edition.

ISO/IEC 17024 is now in its third edition, ISO/IEC 17024:2026 (published March 2026). Any gap analysis uses that edition, and transition dates are confirmed with the accreditation body directly.

## Q4. English only, or other languages?
**Recommendation:**
- **English is the language of record:** credentials, schemes, standards and the authoritative text of every publication.
- **Arabic is the first service language:** key public pages (M.1) and Arabic editions of the library and publications (M.4), translated by qualified people. A line on each Arabic edition names the English version as the authoritative text.
- **Exams in Arabic only later,** after the English scheme is stable, and only through the ITC Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Tests [C16]: double translation with reconciliation, equivalence evidence and piloting.

Practice of comparable bodies:
- The IIA offers the CIA exam in Arabic and 13 other languages and states that the English version is authoritative [C7, C15].
- SHRM offers its exams and body of knowledge in Arabic to residents of listed Arab countries, including all six GCC states [C15].
- ACAMS offers CAMS in Arabic and other languages, and its advanced credential in English only [C6].
- ICA offers Arabic certificates, introduced "in response to feedback from regulators around the region" [C15].
- IAPP translates "from our English exam", with no machine translation [C15].
- CGI UK & Ireland teaches and examines in English only [C2].

## Q5. Show the MENA focus from day one?
**Recommendation:** yes, but as a region LIV serves, not as LIV's identity. The homepage and the credentials stay international and U.S.-based. A regional hub carries the MENA focus. Comparable U.S. and UK bodies do this without weakening their global standing:
- **SHRM (U.S.):** a "Global / India / MENA" region selector and a MENA subsite with Arabic articles, regional programs and offices in Riyadh and Dubai [C17].
- **CIPD (UK):** separate UK, international and Middle East editions of its site, with global branding on the main site [C18].
- **ICA (UK):** an international homepage and a Middle East landing page mixing English and Arabic [C19].

Applied to LIV:
- **Homepage:** international identity.
- **"Regions" menu:** MENA and GCC hub (English and Arabic) with the GCC regulatory map, the country acceptance guides and regional contacts.
- **Future regions** are added the same way.

This keeps the owner's decision of October 5 (GCC and MENA as the first markets) and LIV's position as an international body.
