Family businesses carry a strength and a risk in the same place: the family. Trust, patience and shared values can carry a company through generations. Unspoken expectations about jobs, money and control can also break it apart. Family business governance exists to keep the strength and manage the risk. It does so by deciding, in advance and in writing, where each kind of decision is taken.

## Three circles, three kinds of decision

The IFC Family Business Governance Handbook describes a family business as the overlap of three groups: the **family**, the **owners** and the **business** (management and employees) [1]. A person can sit in one, two or all three circles, and each circle has its own legitimate interests:

| Circle | Typical questions | Where they are decided |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Values, family employment, education of the next generation, support for family members | Family assembly and family council |
| Ownership | Dividends, sale of shares, new owners, strategy and risk appetite | Shareholders' meeting, owners' agreement |
| Business | Operations, hiring, budgets, performance | Board and management |

Most family business conflicts start when a question from one circle is settled in another, such as a job given because of family ties rather than competence.

## The family institutions

The IFC handbook describes a set of family governance institutions that grow with the family [1]:

- **Family assembly:** a meeting of all family members, often annual, to share information and keep the family united around the business.
- **Family council:** a smaller elected or appointed group that represents the family, prepares policies and connects the family with the board.
- **Family charter (or constitution):** a written statement of the family's values, its vision for the business and its policies. It does not replace the company's legal documents, which still prevail.

A first-generation business with a founder and a few children rarely needs all of these. A third-generation family with dozens of owners usually cannot do without them.

## What a family charter usually covers

1. Family values and the vision for the business
2. Who counts as a family member for the charter's purposes, including spouses
3. Roles of the family assembly, family council and board
4. **Family employment policy:** entry requirements (education, outside experience), the same pay scales and reviews as non-family staff, and who decides
5. **Ownership policy:** who may own shares, transfer rules, rights of first refusal, valuation method
6. **Dividend policy:** how profits are split between reinvestment and distribution
7. **Succession:** how future leaders are prepared and chosen
8. **Conflict resolution:** steps from discussion to mediation, before any legal action
9. How the charter is reviewed and amended

## An effective board

As a family business grows, the board must become a real governing body, not a family meeting under another name. ISO 37000:2021 describes the governing body's role as directing, overseeing and holding the organization accountable for its purpose [4]. Practical steps:

- Separate board meetings from family gatherings, with a written agenda, papers in advance and minutes.
- Add one or two experienced **outside members** who owe no personal loyalty to any branch of the family.
- Set up a board-level review of performance and risk, and of related party dealings between family members and the business.
- Where a formal board is not yet practical, an **advisory board** of outside experts is a proportionate first step. A literature review of governance in small and medium enterprises found that such firms often reach expertise through advisory boards and outside advisers rather than formal boards [5].

## Succession

Succession is the moment most family businesses fail or renew themselves. A study of UAE family firms found that large firms were more aware of generational transition risk and planned for the long term. Medium-sized family firms focused on short-term returns and needed more succession planning, strategic planning and formal governance [2]. Good practice includes:

- Starting the conversation years before the transition, while the founder is active
- Defining the qualities and experience the next leader needs, for family and non-family candidates alike
- Giving potential successors experience outside the family business
- Planning the founder's new role: chair, adviser or ambassador
- Writing down the plan, including what happens if a key person dies or falls ill suddenly

## Evidence from the GCC

Research on listed companies in the GCC found that private family control was associated with weaker environmental, social and governance (ESG) performance. The association weakened markedly as family firms aged, which suggests that governance matures over time [3]. The authors suggest that support should focus on younger family firms [3]. The study covers listed firms only, so its findings cannot be applied directly to private family businesses.

## Getting started

1. Draw the three circles and place every family member and owner in them.
2. List the decisions that cause the most disagreement and agree in which circle each belongs.
3. Write a family employment policy first, because it is usually the most urgent.
4. Hold a first family assembly to share the business's position and the plan.
5. Add an outside adviser or board member within the next year.

*General guidance drawing on the sources cited. Family charters interact with company, inheritance and contract law, which differ by country. Take legal advice before adopting one.
