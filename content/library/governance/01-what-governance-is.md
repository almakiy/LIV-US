Most people meet governance as a set of documents: a board charter, a delegation of authority, a policy manual. Those documents matter, but they are not governance itself. Governance is the system that decides **who sets direction, who checks that the direction is followed, and who answers for the results**. This explainer sets out the concept as two widely used international references describe it, before any question of training or certification arises.

## The two reference points

**ISO 37000:2021, Governance of organizations: Guidance** is the first international standard written specifically about governance as a whole [1]. It is guidance, not a requirements standard, so there is nothing to "certify" against. It is addressed to governing bodies and governing groups, and it applies to every type of organization: companies, family businesses, government entities, non-profits, cooperatives and small enterprises [1].

**The G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance**, revised in 2023, are the main international benchmark that policymakers use when they write company law and listing rules [2]. They are aimed mainly at publicly traded companies. Unlisted and family companies still use them as a reference.

## A working definition

ISO 37000 treats governance as a human-based system by which an organization is **directed, overseen and held accountable** for achieving its defined purpose [1]. Three verbs carry the idea:

| Function | Question it answers | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Where are we going, and within what limits? | Purpose statement, strategy, risk appetite, policies |
| Oversee | Is it happening as intended? | Board reports, internal control, audits, indicators |
| Be accountable | Who answers, to whom, for what? | Delegation of authority, disclosures, minutes, decisions on the record |

## Governance is not management

The most common confusion is between governing and managing. Management runs the organization day to day, within the direction and limits that governance sets. Governance decides the direction and the limits, and it checks how management uses the authority it has been given [1][2].

| Governance | Management |
|---|---|
| Sets purpose, strategy and risk appetite | Plans and delivers within them |
| Delegates authority and sets limits | Uses the delegated authority |
| Oversees performance, conduct and risk | Reports performance, conduct and risk |
| Answers to owners and stakeholders | Answers to the governing body |

In a small business, the owner often does both jobs. The distinction still helps. An owner who sets aside one hour a month to *govern*, reviewing the numbers, the risks and the decisions taken by others, runs a better business than one who only *manages*.

## The ISO 37000 principles

ISO 37000 organizes good governance into eleven principles [1]:

- **Primary principle:** *Purpose.* The organization knows why it exists and what value it is meant to create.
- **Foundational principles:** *Value generation*, *Strategy*, *Oversight* and *Accountability.*
- **Enabling principles:** *Stakeholder engagement*, *Leadership*, *Data and decisions*, *Risk governance*, *Social responsibility*, and *Viability and performance over time.*

The order matters. Purpose comes first: if the organization cannot say what it is for, it has no basis for judging strategy, risk or performance.

## What the G20/OECD Principles add

The 2023 Principles are organized in six chapters [2]:

1. Ensuring the basis for an effective corporate governance framework
2. The rights and equitable treatment of shareholders and key ownership functions
3. Institutional investors, stock markets and other intermediaries
4. Disclosure and transparency
5. The responsibilities of the board
6. Sustainability and resilience [2]

The sixth chapter is new in the 2023 revision [2]. It reflects the expectation that boards consider climate and other sustainability risks as part of their ordinary oversight duties.

## How codes put the principles to work

Many countries turn these principles into a national code. The UK Corporate Governance Code 2024 is one example. It works on a "comply or explain" basis: listed companies either follow each provision or explain why they do not [5]. Its sections cover board leadership and company purpose, division of responsibilities, composition and evaluation, audit, risk and internal control, and remuneration [5]. The structure of almost every national code follows the same logic. The Gulf codes are mapped in a separate item in this collection.

## Measuring governance

Two companion standards help an organization see how far it has come:

- **ISO 37004:2023** describes a governance maturity model, so an organization can locate itself and plan the next step [3].
- **ISO 37005:2024** gives guidance on developing indicators that show whether governance is effective [4].

## Five common misconceptions

1. *"Governance is only for big companies."* ISO 37000 is written for organizations of every size [1]. A shop with five staff has owners, decisions, money and risks, and so it has governance, good or bad.
2. *"Governance means more paperwork."* The aim is clear authority and honest oversight. Paper is only the record of them.
3. *"Governance is the same as compliance."* Compliance is one of the things governance oversees. Governance also covers purpose, strategy, value and stakeholders.
4. *"A board guarantees good governance."* A board that never challenges management, or that sees only good news, is a formality.
5. *"Once written, it is done."* Governance needs review. Maturity models and indicators exist because it changes as the organization grows [3][4].
