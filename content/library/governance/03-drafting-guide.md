A governance framework is a small set of documents that together answer one question: *who may decide what, within which limits, and how do we know it worked?* This guide takes you from a blank page to a first complete version. It follows the five layers described in the companion framework, and each step names the reference it draws on.

## Before you write: three rules

1. **Law first, then your choices.** Company law, the articles of association and any sector or listing rules set the floor. Your framework can add to them, but it must never contradict them. Where a rule is legally binding, cite the law. Where it is your own choice, say so.
2. **One fact, one place.** A spending limit should appear in one document, the delegation of authority, and be referred to everywhere else. Repeated rules drift apart.
3. **Write for the person at the counter.** If a branch manager cannot tell from the text whether they may approve a refund, the text has failed.

## Step 1: Map your obligations

List what the organization must comply with: its legal form and registration, licenses and permits, tax, labor and safety law, data protection, sector rules and contracts. ISO 37301:2021 calls these *compliance obligations*. It covers both requirements the organization must meet and commitments it chooses to make [2]. Record for each one its source, the owner inside the organization and the review date.

## Step 2: State the purpose

Write a short statement of why the organization exists, whom it serves and what it will not do. ISO 37000:2021 places purpose first because every later judgment about strategy, risk and performance depends on it [1].

## Step 3: Write the governance charter

The charter is the constitution of your governance. Keep it short, between two and six pages:

- Who the owners are, and how they take decisions together
- The governing body (board, owners' council or sole owner): its membership, chair, meetings, quorum, voting and records
- The relationship between the governing body and the chief executive or general manager
- How the charter itself is changed

For companies, align the charter with the articles of association and the company law. The G20/OECD Principles describe the board's core responsibilities. Among them are reviewing strategy, risk policy and major plans, and selecting and overseeing key executives [3].

## Step 4: List the reserved matters

Reserved matters are the decisions that only the owners or the governing body may take. Typical examples:

- Changes to ownership, capital or legal form
- Approval of strategy, annual budget and major investments above a set amount
- Borrowing, guarantees and security over assets
- Appointment and removal of the chief executive
- Entering a new country or a new line of business
- Related party transactions above a set amount
- Approval of the annual accounts and dividends

## Step 5: Build the delegation of authority

Everything not reserved is delegated. A delegation of authority matrix shows, for each type of decision, who may *propose*, who must be *consulted*, who *approves* and up to what *amount*, and who must be *informed* afterward. Keep it to the decisions that carry money, legal or reputational risk. A starter template appears in this collection.

## Step 6: Set up committees only where they help

Committees exist to give a subject more time and expertise than the full governing body can. Each needs written terms of reference covering purpose, membership, authority, meetings and reporting. Typical committees in larger organizations are audit, risk, nomination and remuneration. A small business usually needs none. A monthly owners' review covers the same ground.

## Step 7: Organize the document hierarchy

| Level | Answers | Approved by | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy | What we commit to, and why | Governing body | Anti-bribery policy |
| Standard or rule | The fixed requirements | Executive | Gifts above a set value must be declared |
| Procedure | How to do it, step by step | Process owner | How to record a gift in the register |
| Record | Proof that it was done | Generated by the work | The gift register entry |

Give every document an owner, a version number, an approval date and a next review date. A small register of controlled documents is enough.

## Step 8: Write the conduct rules that protect decisions

Three rules protect the integrity of decisions in every organization:

- **Conflicts of interest:** declared before the decision, recorded, and the conflicted person stays out of the decision.
- **Related party transactions:** the G20/OECD Principles expect them to be approved and disclosed in a way that protects the company and its shareholders [3].
- **Gifts and hospitality:** clear limits and a register.

## Step 9: Connect oversight

Decide how the governing body will see what is happening:

- A **risk register** reviewed on a set cycle, using the principles and process of ISO 31000:2018 [4]
- **Internal controls** for money, stock, data and safety, organized around the five COSO components [5]
- A **reporting calendar**: what reaches the governing body monthly, quarterly and yearly
- **Independent assurance**: an internal auditor, an external reviewer or, at minimum, an outside accountant

## Step 10: Plan the review

Set a date, normally once a year, to ask: did the framework work? Which decisions fell between the cracks? What changed in the organization or in the law? Record the answers and the changes made.

## Drafting rules for clear, enforceable text

- **Use defined verbs consistently.** Write "must" for a requirement, "should" for a recommendation and "may" for a permission, and say so at the start of the document.
- **Name a role, not a person.** Write "the Finance Manager approves", not "Ahmed approves".
- **Put numbers in one table.** Limits, thresholds and deadlines belong in the delegation of authority or an appendix, not scattered through the text.
- **Say what happens when the rule is broken.** Record the exception, report it to the named role, and state who decides on the consequence.
- **Date and version everything.** An undated policy cannot be enforced with confidence.
- **Avoid copying another organization's framework.** Their structure, risks and law are not yours. Use it for ideas, then write your own.

## A minimum complete set

For a small or mid-size organization, a first complete framework is often no more than:

1. Purpose statement (one page)
2. Governance charter (two to six pages)
3. Reserved matters and delegation of authority (one table)
4. Conflict of interest, gifts and related party policy (two pages)
5. Risk register and internal control checklist
6. Obligations register and compliance calendar
7. Speak-up procedure (one page)
8. Reporting calendar and annual review record

*General guidance drawing on the sources cited. Company law and sector rules differ by country and change over time: check the current official texts, and take legal advice before adopting governance documents. Standards are summarized, not reproduced.*
