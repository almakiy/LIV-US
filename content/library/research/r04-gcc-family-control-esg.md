**Study:** Nimer, Abughazaleh, Tahat and Hossain (2025), *Family Business, ESG, and Firm Age in the GCC Corporations: Building on the Socioemotional Wealth (SEW) Model*, Journal of Risk and Financial Management 18(5), 241 [1].

## The question

Does private family control affect the environmental, social and governance (ESG) performance of listed companies in the GCC? Does the age of the firm change that effect [1]? The study excludes state and royal holdings from its definition of family control.

## Method and sample

Pooled regression analysis of 2,382 firm-year observations of GCC listed companies from 2016 to 2021, with ESG scores from LSEG (formerly Refinitiv) [1]. Saudi Arabian firms make up about 44% of the observations. A firm counts as family-controlled when one family holds at least 25% of it. On that definition, 15.3% of the sample is family-controlled [1].

## Key findings

- **Weaker ESG under private family control.** Private family control has a robust negative association with ESG performance. The authors read this as family goals taking precedence over wider stakeholder goals, in line with the socioemotional wealth view [1].
- **Age changes the picture.** The negative effect weakens markedly as family firms grow older. The authors suggest that governance matures and that reputation and legacy matter more over time [1].
- **Where to focus support.** The authors suggest that policy and support should prioritize younger private family firms [1].

## Limitations

- Only listed firms with LSEG ESG scores are included, which introduces selection bias [1].
- ESG scores depend on disclosure and tend to favor larger firms [1].
- ESG reporting was voluntary in most GCC markets during the study period [1].
- The findings do not extend to private, unlisted family businesses [1].

## What it means for practitioners

- Younger family-controlled companies, especially those preparing to list or raise capital, gain most from early investment in governance, disclosure and stakeholder engagement.
- The G20/OECD Principles include a chapter on sustainability and resilience. It reflects the growing expectation that boards oversee ESG matters [2].
- Families should not read the findings as a verdict on family ownership. They describe an average association, and the effect fades as governance matures.
