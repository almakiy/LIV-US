Board independence is one of the main tools of governance codes, including the Corporate Governance Regulations issued by Saudi Arabia's Capital Market Authority [4]. This digest reads three open-access studies side by side, because each on its own gives only part of the picture.

## The studies

| Study | Sample and period | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Chebbi and Ammer (2022) [1] | 38 Saudi listed firms, 241 firm-years, 2015 to 2021 | Panel regressions on Bloomberg ESG disclosure scores |
| Bchennaty and Khan (2026) [2] | 164 non-financial listed firms, 1,476 firm-years, 2015 to 2024, plus interviews with 28 governance actors | Mixed methods |
| Al-Faryan and Alokla (2023) [3] | 35 listed insurers, 2008 to 2014 | Several panel methods, including difference-in-differences around a 2009 regulatory change |

## Key findings

- **Independence and disclosure.** Larger and more independent boards were associated with more ESG disclosure. The governance reforms strengthened the effect of board size and independence on disclosure [1]. Board gender diversity was positive but not statistically significant overall [1].
- **Reform and accountability.** The period after the reforms was associated with more accountability, greater board independence, stronger committees and more board gender diversity [2]. Concentrated ownership weakened the link between reform and accountability [2].
- **Independence in practice.** Interviews showed that formally independent directors did not always behave independently or scrutinize management closely [2]. The authors conclude that adopting codes on paper is not enough: what counts is how directors actually exercise scrutiny [2].
- **A counterweight from insurance.** In listed insurers, the 2009 rule change on board composition and audit committee size had a positive effect on financial performance. Adding further independent directors and audit committee members was associated with weaker performance [3].

## Limitations

- The disclosure study covered only 38 firms with Bloomberg scores, and its period largely predates the Saudi Exchange's ESG disclosure guidelines [1].
- The accountability study has no untreated control group. It cannot attribute effects to the regulation itself, and its period overlaps other market changes [2]. It was published two days before this digest was prepared and has no post-publication record yet.
- The insurance study uses data that are now more than ten years old and covers a single sector [3].
- None of the three studies covers unlisted or family companies outside the stock market.

## What it means for practitioners

- Independence is necessary but not sufficient. Boards should judge independence by behavior: the questions directors ask, the information they demand and their willingness to disagree.
- Where one shareholder or family holds a large stake, protections for minority shareholders and genuinely independent directors matter most.
- More independent seats are not automatically better for performance. Composition should match the company's needs and expertise gaps.
- The binding requirements for listed companies are in the CMA's Corporate Governance Regulations. Read them in their current official text [4].
