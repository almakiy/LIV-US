Two UK government texts describe how a board should work, and both are openly licensed. One is written for the boards of central government departments [1]. The other is a pair of guides for the boards of academy trusts and maintained schools [2][3]. Their settings differ, but the board practices they set out apply to almost any organization.

## The two texts at a glance

| | Central government code | Department for Education guides |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Corporate governance in central government departments: code of good practice [1] | Academy trusts: governance guide [2]; Maintained schools: governance guide [3] |
| Issued by | HM Treasury and Cabinet Office [1] | Department for Education [2][3] |
| Edition | April 2017, updating the 2011 editions; a separate guidance note goes with it [1] | First published November 19, 2025. The July 15, 2026 update reflects new duties from September 1, 2026; the academy guide had a further update on July 20, 2026 [2][3] |
| Written for | Departmental boards chaired by Secretaries of State, with ministers, civil servants and non-executive members [1] | Trustees, governors, executive leaders and governance professionals in England [2][3] |
| Structure | Six chapters: parliamentary accountability, role of the board, board composition, board effectiveness, risk management, arm's length bodies [1] | Seven numbered sections each. The academy guide covers culture and engagement, governance of the trust, strategy, non-executive leadership, executive leadership, accountability and compliance [2] |

The school guides describe themselves as non-statutory guidance [2][3]. The maintained schools guide arranges its seven sections differently: effective governance, strategic leadership, accountability, people, governance structures, evaluation and compliance [3].

## Comply or explain

The central government code is "based on principles" [1]. Departures "may be justified if good governance can be achieved by other means" [1]. The reasons go in the governance statement that comes with the annual accounts. A department should show how its practice is still consistent with the principle and supports good governance [1]. The code puts it simply: "One size never fits all" [1].

The code is also modest about what rules can do. It says compliance "cannot guarantee good performance but it does make it more likely" [1]. HM Treasury's Orange Book applies the same comply-or-explain approach to risk management [4].

**Lesson for any board:** decide which practices you follow, and say so once a year. Where you depart from one, explain why and what you do instead.

## The must/should drafting convention

Both school guides define their verbs at the start [2][3]:
- "must" marks a legal or regulatory requirement or duty;
- "should" marks the minimum good practice, unless an alternative approach can be shown to suit the circumstances better;
- where neither word is used, the text reflects a generally held view of high-quality governance.

The central government code uses a similar three-level layout. Each chapter has principles, a "government policy" part in most chapters, and supporting provisions [1].

**Lesson for any board:** label your own board manual the same way. Keep legal duties, house rules and plain advice apart. Members then know where they have room to adapt.

## The board's role

The code says each department "should have an effective board" [1]. The board operates collectively. It advises on strategy and operations, and it scrutinizes and challenges policy and performance with the long term in mind (2.1) [1]. It works to four precepts drawn from business: leadership, effectiveness, accountability and sustainability (2.3) [1].

Under paragraph 2.4, the board meets at least quarterly, and more often as best practice [1]. It covers five areas: strategic clarity, commercial sense, talented people, results focus and management information [1]. Paragraph 2.8 asks the board to write a board operating framework. That framework includes a formal schedule of matters reserved for the board and is reviewed at least every two years [1]. Board members record unresolved concerns in the minutes (2.12) [1].

The school guides stress the same split between governing and managing. For academy trusts, governance exists to provide "strategic leadership", "accountability and assurance" and "strategic engagement" (2.2) [2]. The board can delegate decisions to committees. It "cannot delegate responsibility and must retain overall control" (4.3) [2]. A scheme of delegation must set out the remit of members, the board and each committee (4.3.6) [2]. Academy trust boards must meet at least 3 times a year (4.3.3) [2].

For maintained schools, the core functions include a clear vision and strategic direction. They also include making sure the headteacher delivers the school's educational performance, and the sound use of its money (1.1) [3]. Governors use their skills to oversee, not to do the work. The guide's example: someone with financial skills should scrutinize the accounts but not help prepare them (4.1) [3].

## Composition and effectiveness

**Composition.** The code asks for a balance of skills and experience, and a board that is "balanced, diverse and manageable in size" (3.1) [1]. A departmental board should include at least four non-executive members and a professionally qualified finance director (3.3) [1]. The skills mix is reviewed at least annually as part of the board evaluation (3.12) [1]. Appointments are made on merit, with regard for the benefits of diversity (3.13) [1].

The academy guide says the department prefers smaller boards that are cohesive and decisive (4.3.1) [2]. The board should identify the skills it needs, including enough financial knowledge to hold the executive to account (2.4) [2]. A diverse board with no "inappropriately close personal or business ties" helps ensure enough challenge (2.4) [2]. A maintained school governing body must have no fewer than 7 governors (5.2) [3].

**Terms and roles.** In academy trusts, trustee terms run between 1 and 4 years (4.3.7) [2]. The board elects its chair each school year (4.4.2) [2]. The department recommends that trustees serve on no more than 2 boards, except in exceptional circumstances (4.4.4) [2].

**Board support.** The code calls an effective board secretary essential. The secretary sets the agenda with the chair, checks board papers and records decisions (4.11) [1]. The school guides require a governance professional, sometimes called the clerk [2][3]. In an academy trust this person is not a trustee, principal or senior executive leader (4.5.1) [2]. In a maintained school the clerk cannot be a governor or the headteacher (4.8) [3].

**Evaluation.** The code asks for a "formal and rigorous annual evaluation" of the board, its committees and each member (4.1) [1]. It adds independent input at least once every three years (4.12) [1]. Attendance at board and committee meetings is disclosed in the governance statement (4.6) [1]. The academy guide notes that two sector codes recommend external evaluations every 3 years for large charities (4.9) [2]. Both school guides say an external review "is not an audit or inspection" [2][3]. The maintained schools guide adds that reviews matter most at growth or transition points (section 6) [3].

## Committees

The code expects at least an audit and risk assurance committee and a nominations committee (2.5) [1]. Under paragraph 2.10, committees exercise governance functions only and do not stray into executive management [1]. A non-executive chairs each one, with terms of reference agreed by the board [1]. The board receives timely feedback from them, and the delegations are written down [1].

The audit and risk assurance committee has at least three members. At least one, and preferably more, has recent and relevant financial experience (5.9) [1]. It has no executive role and does not make or endorse decisions (5.11) [1]. Its terms of reference are public, and its work is reported each year (5.14) [1]. The nominations committee is advisory. It scrutinizes leadership development, succession, and incentives and rewards, but does not decide individual cases (4.4 and 4.5) [1].

The academy guide sets similar rules (4.3.4) [2]. Each committee other than a local committee must have a majority of trustees, and people from outside the board may join. The board must have an audit and risk committee and should have a finance committee. The two can be combined where annual income is below £50 million [2]. At least one audit and risk committee member should have recent or relevant experience in accountancy or audit assurance [2].

## Risk

The code is clear that risk belongs to the whole board. "Advice about and scrutiny of key risks is a matter for the board, not a committee." (5.1) [1]. The board gives "a clear steer on the desired risk appetite" (5.6) [1]. It also checks that controls are prudent, that accountability for each risk is clear, and that staff have the skills they need [1]. Risk is a regular board agenda item (5.3), and the head of internal audit attends from time to time (5.5) [1]. The code points to the Orange Book for risk appetite [1][4].

Both school guides say managing risk is essential to meet key objectives and to protect funds and assets [2][3]. An academy trust board must set policies that support the independent and effective use of internal and external audit (6.4) [2]. It assures itself of the integrity of financial information, robust risk controls and compliance (section 6) [2]. A maintained school governing body oversees a whole-school approach to risk. It keeps a framework for recording risks and incidents and reviews all risks regularly (2.5) [3].

Conflicts of interest are part of risk. In academy trusts, conflicts "must be avoided or managed" (6.5) [2]. In maintained schools, a governor with a conflict must disclose it, withdraw from the meeting and not vote (2.4) [3]. The code asks the board to keep a system for recording conflicts and to publish members' relevant interests (4.15) [1]. Academy trusts must also have whistleblowing procedures. They should name at least one trustee and one staff member to receive concerns (7.19) [2].

## Checklist: board practices any organization can adopt

These practices are adapted from the texts for a nonprofit, a family firm or a small company. The numbers in brackets are the paragraphs or sections of the source.

- Write a short board charter with a list of matters reserved for the board, and review it at least every two years (code 2.8) [1].
- Label every rule in the board manual as a legal duty ("must"), a house rule ("should") or advice [2][3].
- Report once a year on how you follow your chosen practices, and explain each departure (comply or explain) [1].
- Meet on a fixed cycle. The code sets at least quarterly; the academy guide sets at least 3 times a year [1][2].
- Keep directing and managing apart. The board sets direction and scrutinizes; managers run the business [1][3].
- Keep a skills matrix, review it every year and recruit for the gaps, with diversity in mind [1][2].
- Bring in at least one outside voice, such as a non-family director or an independent trustee, as the code does with non-executive members [1].
- Set up an audit and risk committee, or a standing board agenda item if you are small. Include at least one member with financial experience and give it no executive role [1][2].
- Agree a risk appetite statement, and keep scrutiny of key risks at full board level [1][4].
- Keep a register of interests; a conflicted member declares, leaves the room and does not vote [1][3].
- Appoint a board secretary who is not the chair or the chief executive [2][3].
- Give every new member an induction and set a small training budget (code 4.1; academy guide 4.7) [1][2].
- Evaluate the board every year, with an outside review every three years or before a major change [1][2].
- Minute decisions, actions and any unresolved concerns (code 2.12) [1].
- Write a scheme of delegation, and name a board member to receive whistleblowing concerns [2].
