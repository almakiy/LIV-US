The Orange Book is HM Treasury's guidance on risk management in UK government. The current text, "Management of Risk – Principles and Concepts", is the May 2023 edition [1]. It applies to government departments and arm's length public bodies that are responsible for public funds. The book also says it "may be useful to all parts of the UK public sector" [1].

The principles give the "what" and the "why", not the "how" [1]. Each organization designs its own policies and procedures. That is what makes the book useful outside government. The book itself warns that a one-size-fits-all approach "would be misguided", because organizations differ in size, structure and needs [1].

## How the document is organized

The 2023 edition has two parts and six annexes [1]:

| Part or annex | Content |
|---|---|
| Part I: Risk Management Principles | Sections A to E, one for each main principle, each with numbered supporting principles |
| Part II: The Risk Control Framework | Section F (structure of the framework) and Section G (assurance) |
| Annex 1 | Roles and responsibilities of the board, the accounting officer and the Audit and Risk Assurance Committee |
| Annex 2 | The three lines model |
| Annex 3 | Questions to ask (35 questions grouped under the five principles) |
| Annex 4 | Example risk categories |
| Annex 5 | Definitions and supportive concepts |
| Annex 6 | References |

The 2023 edition builds on the 2020 version. Its main addition is Part II [1].

## How to read the verbs

The book opens with a table of drafting verbs [1]:

| Term | Meaning in the Orange Book |
|---|---|
| "shall" | "a requirement: a mandatory element" |
| "should" | "a recommendation: an advisory element" |
| "may" | "approval" |
| "might" | "a possibility" |
| "can" | "both capability and possibility" |
| "is/are" | "a description" |

The main principles use "shall". The supporting principles use "should". The book calls the main principles "mandatory requirements" and the supporting principles "advisory" [1].

## Comply or explain

The Orange Book works on a comply-or-explain basis [1]. When the board reviews its risk arrangements and prepares the governance statement, it considers whether the main principles are met. The supporting principles inform that judgment. Departures "may be justified if good risk management can be achieved by other means" [1].

Each government organization either discloses compliance or explains its departures in the governance statement that comes with its annual accounts [1]. The book says this keeps flexibility while letting stakeholders hold leaders to account. The central government corporate governance code uses the same approach for boards [2].

## The five main principles (Part I)

For the risk management framework to be considered effective, the book says five principles "shall be applied" [1]. The table restates each one in plain words.

| Principle | Section | What it says | What it asks in practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Governance and Leadership | Risk management is an essential part of governance and leadership. It is fundamental to how the organization is directed, managed and controlled at all levels [1]. | The board leads on risk, sets the risk appetite and owns the risk culture. |
| B | Integration | Risk management is an integral part of all activities "to support decision-making in achieving objectives" [1]. | Risk is part of strategy, planning, option appraisal, budgets and performance reviews, not a separate exercise. |
| C | Collaboration and Best Information | "Risk management shall be collaborative and informed by the best available information and expertise." [1] | Use experts, partners and stakeholders. Look across the whole supply chain and partner network. |
| D | Risk Management Processes | Processes include (a) risk identification and assessment; (b) selecting and implementing risk treatment; (c) integrated, insightful risk monitoring; and (d) timely, accurate and useful risk reporting [1]. | Run a cycle: identify, assess, treat, monitor and report, then repeat. |
| E | Continual Improvement | "Risk management shall be continually improved through learning and experience." [1] | Review the framework, learn lessons and act on them. |

## The supporting principles in summary

Each section adds numbered supporting principles [1]. They name who should do what, mostly the board and the accounting officer. The accounting officer is the senior executive official of a public body [1].

| Section | Supporting principles | Main points |
|---|---|---|
| A | A1 to A10 | Governance suited to the organization's business, scale and culture (A1). Periodic checks that leadership style and people policies support the desired risk culture (A2). The board sets the risk appetite: the nature and extent of the principal risks it is willing to take (A3). Clear roles, for which the three lines model may be used (A4). Agreed triggers to bring significant issues to the board quickly (A5). Balanced reports on principal risks (A6). A designated, senior person leads risk management (A8). Resources for risk work (A9). A risk policy or statement, reviewed periodically (A10) [1]. |
| B | B1 to B5 | Risk is embedded in strategy, programs and projects, resources, operations, performance and assets (B1). Option appraisal looks at scenarios, assumptions, optimism or status quo bias and links between risks (B2). Principal risks are visible in business and financial plans (B3). Horizon scanning and scenario planning (B4). Risks to the public (B5) [1]. |
| C | C1 to C6 | Cover all types and sources of risk, including the "extended enterprise" of partners and suppliers (C1). Relationships with arm's length bodies (C2). Systematic, iterative and collaborative processes that draw on experts (C3). Consultation and communication with stakeholders (C4, C5). Functions give expert advice (C6) [1]. |
| D | D1 to D16 | A holistic view of risks, often by category, whether or not their sources are under the organization's control (D2). Common risk criteria based on likelihood and consequences (D3 to D5). Evaluation against risk appetite, with options to avoid, take or increase, retain, change the likelihood, change the consequences, or share the risk (D6). Treatment plans that name actions, owners, resources, measures and dates (D7, D8). Contingency and continuity arrangements (D9). Monitoring, including the three lines, with no gaps and no unnecessary duplication (D10 to D12). The board specifies the information it needs, and reports show whether risks are within appetite (D13 to D15). "Deep dive" reviews of principal risks (D16) [1]. |
| E | E1 to E4 | A review of the framework and its results at least annually (E1). Evaluation and lessons learned (E2). Maturity models may be used as a benchmark (E3). Improvement tasks assigned to accountable people (E4) [1]. |

## The Risk Control Framework (Part II)

Part II "does not introduce any additional principles" [1]. It adds detail on how risk is controlled and stays non-prescriptive about methods. Its purpose is to give structure to existing control requirements. This helps accounting officers trust their controls and choose which improvements come first [1]. The book notes that better control "can allow for a higher level of risk to be taken where desired" [1].

The Treasury Officer of Accounts built the framework with the Government Internal Audit Agency. In summer 2022 the first Head of the Government Risk Profession took custody of it [1]. It has four pillars, each with blocks, and one underpinning requirement [1]:

| Pillar | Blocks | What it expects |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Governance and Management Framework | Propriety and ethics; governance statement and accounting officer system statement; boards; arm's length bodies and joint ventures | A governance framework that meets expected standards of conduct, efficiency and transparency |
| 2. Roles and Accountabilities | Accounting officer; all staff; functional roles; senior responsible owners for major projects | Roles assigned to people with the right seniority, skills and experience; everyone clear on their part in managing risks and controls |
| 3. Strategy, Planning and Reporting | Medium-term planning; annual planning; processes; reporting | Short, medium and longer-term planning that keeps risks to strategy visible; reporting that supports risk-based decisions |
| 4. Standards, Policies and Procedures | Delegations and budgetary control; functional standards; public sector and wider statutory requirements; organizational policies and procedures | Timely approvals within delegations of authority; local controls that reflect the scale, nature and complexity of the organization |
| Underpinning requirement | The three lines model | Effective culture and operation of the three lines, including appropriate assurance |

Section G covers assurance. It describes assurance mapping, which links sources of assurance to the risks that threaten objectives [1]. It lists four approaches used in government: principal risk based, process based, control based, and risk assurance. They can be combined [1]. Its tips include starting with what matters most strategically, reusing existing risk information, and keeping assurance proportionate [1].

The publication page now carries the Central Government's Assurance Directory. On April 24, 2025 it replaced the former bank of risk management questions [1].

## Roles and the three lines

Annex 1 sets out roles [1]:
- **The board**, advised by its Audit and Risk Assurance Committee, leads the assessment and management of risk. It sets the risk appetite and specifies the information it needs. It assures itself that the framework works, and explains any departures from the Corporate Governance Code in the governance statement [1][2].
- **The accounting officer** shapes the risk culture, designates a senior risk lead and sets the overall approach. The accounting officer also secures resources and checks the quality of risk information [1].
- **The Audit and Risk Assurance Committee** supports the board. It critically challenges and reviews the risk framework and controls "without second guessing management" [1].

Annex 2 explains the three lines model. It is a flexible structure, not a blueprint for an organization chart [1]. Governing bodies and senior management are not among the lines. They are the main stakeholders the lines serve [1].

| Line | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| First line | Management | Owns and manages risks, runs internal controls day to day and fixes deficiencies [1] |
| Second line | Functions that oversee or specialize in risk | Set standards and policies, help management design controls, monitor compliance and alert leaders to emerging issues [1] |
| Third line | Internal audit | Gives an objective evaluation of governance, risk management and control, independent from management [1] |

Outside the lines sit external sources of assurance. In UK government these include the National Audit Office and the Infrastructure and Projects Authority [1].

The other annexes are practical tools. Annex 3 lists 35 self-assessment questions under the five principles [1]. Annex 4 gives example risk categories, from strategy and governance risks to security and reputational risks [1]. Annex 5 defines risk as "the effect of uncertainty on objectives". It suggests stating each risk as cause, event and consequence [1].

## The supplementary guides

The 2023 text lists four supplementary guides that support the book [1]:
- Risk Appetite Guidance Note.
- Risk Management Skills and Capabilities Framework.
- Risk Reporting Good Practice Guide.
- Portfolio Risk Management Guidance.

These guides were withdrawn on July 29, 2026. The publication page records that they "were deleted as they were out of date" and contained links to superseded guidance [1]. The main Orange Book text remains published. This item therefore does not draw on the four guides.

## Using it in a small or family business

A small firm has no accounting officer and no audit committee. The ideas still work at a smaller scale. The table gives one practical version; these suggestions are ours, not the book's.

| Orange Book idea | Small or family business version |
|---|---|
| The board leads on risk (Principle A) | Put the top risks on the agenda of every owners' or family council meeting |
| Risk appetite (A3) | Write one page on which risks you will take, such as a new product, and which you will not, such as safety or running short of cash |
| A designated risk lead (A8) | Name one person, for example the finance manager, to keep the risk register up to date |
| Escalation triggers (A5) | Agree in advance what goes to the owner the same day, such as a lost major customer or a serious incident |
| Cause, event, consequence (Annex 5) | Keep a one-page register: risk, cause, consequence, owner, action and due date |
| Three lines (Annex 2) | Managers run controls; an outside accountant checks them; an independent reviewer looks at the whole system every few years |
| Assurance mapping (Section G) | A simple grid of top risks against who checks each one and how often |
| Annual review (E1) | Once a year, review the register, the incidents and the lessons, and update the risk policy |
| Comply or explain | If you skip a practice, write down why and what you do instead |
