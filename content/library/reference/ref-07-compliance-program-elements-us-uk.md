Four official texts describe what a good compliance program looks like. Two are American: the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines for organizations [1] and the Justice Department's guide for prosecutors [2]. Two are British: the Ministry of Justice guidance on bribery prevention [3] and the Home Office guidance on fraud prevention [4]. They use different words, but they cover much the same ground. This item sets them side by side and ends with a minimum set for a small business.

## The four texts at a glance

| Text | Issuer and edition | What it is for | How it is built |
|---|---|---|---|
| USSG §8B2.1, Effective Compliance and Ethics Program [1] | U.S. Sentencing Commission, Guidelines Manual of November 1, 2025 | Defines an effective program for the culpability score and the probation conditions when a court sentences an organization | Two aims in (a), seven minimum requirements in (b), periodic risk assessment in (c), then application notes |
| Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs [2] | DOJ Criminal Division, updated September 2024 | Helps prosecutors judge whether a program was effective at the time of the offense and at the time of a charging decision or resolution | Three "fundamental questions", each with sample topics and questions |
| Bribery Act 2010 guidance [3] | UK Ministry of Justice, March 2011 | Issued under section 9 of the Act; explains the procedures that support the defense of "adequate procedures" to the section 7 offense | Six principles, each with commentary, plus case studies |
| Failure to prevent fraud guidance [4] | UK Home Office, dated November 2024; the copy used is version 1.5 | Issued under section 204 of the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023; explains "reasonable" fraud prevention procedures | Six principles in Chapter 3, with examples of good practice |

## U.S. Sentencing Guidelines §8B2.1

Subsection (a) sets two aims. The organization exercises due diligence to prevent and detect criminal conduct. It also promotes "an organizational culture that encourages ethical conduct and a commitment to compliance with the law" [1]. The program must be reasonably designed, implemented and enforced. A failure to prevent or detect one offense does not necessarily mean the program is ineffective [1].

Subsection (b) lists what due diligence and that culture "minimally require" [1]. In plain words, the seven requirements are:

1. **Standards and procedures** to prevent and detect criminal conduct [1].
2. **Oversight and responsibility.** The governing authority knows the program and exercises reasonable oversight. High-level personnel make sure the program is effective, and a specific person among them has overall responsibility. Day-to-day managers report periodically and have adequate resources, appropriate authority and direct access to the governing authority [1].
3. **Care in giving authority.** The organization makes reasonable efforts not to give substantial authority to anyone it knew, or should have known, had engaged in illegal activities or conduct inconsistent with the program [1].
4. **Communication and training** for members of the governing authority, high-level personnel, substantial authority personnel, employees and, as appropriate, agents [1].
5. **Monitoring, evaluation and reporting.** The organization monitors and audits to detect criminal conduct and evaluates the program periodically. It has and publicizes a way to report or seek guidance without fear of retaliation, which may allow anonymity or confidentiality [1].
6. **Incentives and discipline** applied consistently across the organization [1].
7. **Response and prevention.** After criminal conduct is detected, the organization responds appropriately and prevents similar conduct, changing the program where needed [1].

Subsection (c) adds risk assessment. The organization periodically assesses the risk of criminal conduct and designs, implements or modifies each requirement to reduce that risk [1]. Application note 7 says to weigh the nature and seriousness of possible conduct, its likelihood given the business, and the organization's prior history [1]. One example: a business whose sales staff can set prices should have procedures to prevent and detect price-fixing [1].

Application note 2 says each requirement must be met. The specific actions depend on industry practice or regulation, the size of the organization and any similar misconduct [1]. Note 3 adds that day-to-day managers typically report to the governing authority no less than annually, when they are not the person with overall responsibility [1].

## DOJ Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs

The DOJ guide starts from three questions taken from the Justice Manual [2]:

1. "Is the corporation's compliance program well designed?" [2]
2. "Is the program being applied earnestly and in good faith? In other words, is the program adequately resourced and empowered to function effectively?" [2]
3. "Does the corporation's compliance program work in practice?" [2]

The guide stresses that its topics and questions "form neither a checklist nor a formula" [2]. Prosecutors consider the company's size, industry, geographic footprint and regulatory landscape [2]. The main topics under each question are:

| Question | Topics in the guide [2] |
|---|---|
| I. Well designed? | A. Risk assessment; B. Policies and procedures; C. Training and communications; D. Confidential reporting structure and investigation process; E. Third party management; F. Mergers and acquisitions |
| II. Adequately resourced and empowered? | A. Commitment by senior and middle management; B. Autonomy and resources; C. Compensation structures and consequence management |
| III. Works in practice? | A. Continuous improvement, periodic testing and review; B. Investigation of misconduct; C. Analysis and remediation of any underlying misconduct |

The current text also asks how a company manages the risks of new technologies, including artificial intelligence, in its business and in its compliance program [2]. It asks whether compliance staff have timely access to the data they need to monitor and test controls [2]. Under question III, it looks for an honest root cause analysis after misconduct [2].

## The UK guidance: six principles each

Both UK texts rest on six principles. The names are almost the same, but the order differs.

| No. | Bribery Act guidance [3] | Failure to prevent fraud guidance [4] |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Proportionate procedures | Top level commitment |
| 2 | Top-level commitment | Risk assessment |
| 3 | Risk assessment | Proportionate risk-based prevention procedures |
| 4 | Due diligence | Due diligence |
| 5 | Communication (including training) | Communication (including training) |
| 6 | Monitoring and review | Monitoring and review |

Both texts describe the principles as flexible and focused on outcomes [3][4]. Both say procedures should be proportionate to the risks and to the nature, scale and complexity of the organization's activities. Both add that procedures should be "clear, practical, accessible, effectively implemented and enforced" [3][4].

The two texts describe risk assessment in similar terms. The bribery guidance calls it "periodic, informed and documented" [3]. The fraud guidance calls it "dynamic, documented and kept under regular review" [4]. It notes that reviews typically happen annually or once every two years [4]. If an organization decides not to act on a specific risk, the fraud guidance says to record the decision and the name and position of the person who authorized it [4].

Only a court decides whether procedures were adequate or reasonable in a given case [3][4]. In both regimes the organization must prove its procedures on the balance of probabilities [3][4]. A departure from the suggested procedures does not by itself mean the procedures fall short [3][4]. The fraud guidance adds that following it is not a safe harbor [4].

The fraud prevention offense applies only to large organizations [4]. These meet two of three tests: more than 250 employees, more than £36 million turnover, or more than £18 million in total assets [4]. The offense came into effect on September 1, 2025 [4]. The guidance still calls its principles good practice for smaller organizations [4]. It also warns that small suppliers may be "associated persons" of a large client and may face contract terms as a result [4].

The four texts share a point that is easy to miss. None of them treats one incident as proof that a program failed. The Sentencing Guidelines say so in (a) [1], and the DOJ guide repeats it [2]. The bribery guidance states that no policies or procedures can detect and prevent all bribery [3]. What all four look for is a program that fits the real risks, works in practice and improves after problems.

## What a small business does at minimum

The texts expect less formality from a small organization, not less commitment. The Sentencing Guidelines say a small organization must show "the same degree of commitment" as a large one [1]. It may meet the requirements "with less formality and fewer resources" [1]. The bribery guidance says a very small business may rely on periodic oral briefings, while a large one may need extensive written communication [3]. In smaller organizations, top-level managers may need to lead the work personally [3].

Application note 2(C)(iii) gives four examples of how a small organization can do this [1]:

- The governing authority directly manages the compliance and ethics work.
- Employees are trained in informal staff meetings, and monitoring happens through regular "walk-arounds" or continuous observation.
- Existing staff run the program instead of separate compliance staff.
- The program is modeled on well-regarded programs and practices of similar organizations.

On that basis, a minimum program for a small business should cover the following. Each line rests on the texts cited.

| Minimum practice | Basis |
|---|---|
| The owner or board takes charge of compliance and says so to staff | USSG (b)(2) and note 2(C)(iii) [1]; Principle 2 [3] |
| A short written note of the most likely risks, reviewed at regular intervals and after a big change | USSG (c) [1]; Principle 3 [3]; 3.2 [4] |
| A one-page code of conduct covering bribes, gifts, honest dealing with customers and conflicts of interest | USSG (b)(1) and note 7 [1]; I.B [2]; paragraph 1.7 [3] |
| Checks on agents and intermediaries in proportion to the risk they carry | Principle 4 [3]; 3.4 [4] |
| Care before giving authority to anyone with a record of misconduct, applied within employment law | USSG (b)(3) and note 4 [1] |
| Training at staff meetings or short oral briefings | Note 2(C)(iii) [1]; paragraph 1.2 [3] |
| A known way to raise concerns, with confidentiality and no retaliation | USSG (b)(5)(C) [1]; paragraph 5.3 [3] |
| Monitoring by walking around and checking payments and approvals | Note 2(C)(iii) [1]; paragraph 1.7 [3] |
| Fair, consistent discipline and recognition | USSG (b)(6) [1] |
| A response to every problem: fix it, find the cause, adjust the controls | USSG (b)(7) [1]; III.C [2] |
| A record of decisions, including any decision not to act on a risk and who made it | 3.1.2 and 3.3 [4] |

A small business that provides services to a large organization may face its client's compliance terms in contracts [4]. The Sentencing Guidelines likewise encourage large organizations to promote programs among smaller business partners [1].
