Most organizations now use AI in some form, from chat assistants to scoring and monitoring tools. Using AI well is a governance task: someone has to decide where it may be used, check what it does and answer for the results.

This guide draws on four sets of official texts. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework gives a structure [1]. NIST's Generative AI Profile names the risks of generative tools [3]. The UK government's ethics framework and transparency standard give principles and a model public record [4][6]. The EU AI Act shows how one legal system treats AI used in education and exams [8][9]. The guide ends with a starter AI-use policy outline.

## The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)

NIST published the AI RMF 1.0 as NIST AI 100-1 in January 2023 [1]. It was released on January 26, 2023, and is "intended for voluntary use" [2]. NIST's program page states: "The AI RMF 1.0 is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan" [2]. Readers should check that page for a newer edition before relying on the details below.

The framework describes the characteristics of trustworthy AI. They are: valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy enhanced; and fair with harmful biases managed [1].

Its Core has four functions [1]:

| Function | What it does, in NIST's terms |
|---|---|
| GOVERN | A cross-cutting function that is "infused throughout AI risk management and enables the other functions" |
| MAP | "Establishes the context to frame risks related to an AI system" |
| MEASURE | Uses quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods to analyze, assess, benchmark and monitor AI risk and related impacts |
| MANAGE | Allocates risk resources to mapped and measured risks, and plans how to respond to, recover from and communicate about incidents |

Counted from Tables 1 to 4 of AI 100-1, the Core has 19 categories and 72 subcategories. GOVERN has 6 categories and 19 subcategories. MAP has 5 and 18, MEASURE 4 and 22, and MANAGE 4 and 13 [1].

NIST adds that the actions "do not constitute a checklist" [1]. After putting the GOVERN outcomes in place, most users would start with MAP and continue to MEASURE or MANAGE [1].

### The six GOVERN categories

| Category | Plain-words summary | Subcategories |
|---|---|---|
| GOVERN 1: policies, processes, procedures and practices | Know the legal and regulatory requirements. Build trustworthiness into policy. Match the effort to your risk tolerance. Plan monitoring and periodic review. Keep an inventory of AI systems. Retire systems safely. | 7 |
| GOVERN 2: accountability structures | Document roles and lines of communication. Train staff and partners. Executive leadership takes responsibility for decisions about AI risks. | 3 |
| GOVERN 3: workforce diversity, equity, inclusion and accessibility | Let a diverse team inform decisions. Define roles for human-AI configurations and human oversight. | 2 |
| GOVERN 4: a culture that considers and communicates AI risk | Encourage critical thinking and a safety-first mindset. Document risks and impacts. Enable testing, incident identification and information sharing. | 3 |
| GOVERN 5: engagement with relevant AI actors | Collect feedback from people outside the team about impacts. Feed that feedback back into design and use. | 2 |
| GOVERN 6: third-party software, data and supply chain | Address risks from third parties, including infringement of intellectual property. Have contingency plans for failures in high-risk third-party data or AI systems. | 2 |

Two subcategories are good starting points for a small organization. GOVERN 1.6 asks for "mechanisms" to "inventory AI systems" [1]. GOVERN 2.3 asks that executive leadership "takes responsibility for decisions about risks associated with AI system development and deployment" [1].

The AI RMF also uses profiles. A use-case profile applies the framework to one setting, such as hiring. A Current Profile and a Target Profile show where an organization is and where it wants to be [1].

## The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1)

NIST released AI 600-1 on July 26, 2024 [2]. It describes itself as "a cross-sectoral profile of and companion resource for" the AI RMF, for generative AI [3]. It defines risks "unique to or exacerbated by" generative AI and suggests actions to govern, map, measure and manage them [3].

The profile lists 12 risks [3]. In short form:

1. **CBRN information or capabilities:** easier access to information that could help design chemical, biological, radiological or nuclear weapons [3].
2. **Confabulation:** confidently stated but false content, often called "hallucinations" [3].
3. **Dangerous, violent, or hateful content:** easier production of violent, inciting or hateful material, including recommendations to self-harm or to conduct illegal activities [3].
4. **Data privacy:** leakage or unauthorized use or disclosure of personal or sensitive data, including de-anonymization [3].
5. **Environmental impacts:** effects of the heavy computing used to train and run models [3].
6. **Harmful bias or homogenization:** amplified historical and systemic biases, performance gaps between groups or languages, and outputs that are too uniform [3].
7. **Human-AI configuration:** problems in how people and systems interact, such as automation bias, over-reliance or emotional entanglement [3].
8. **Information integrity:** easier creation of content that blurs fact and opinion, or that feeds large-scale disinformation [3].
9. **Information security:** lower barriers to cyberattacks, and new attack surfaces in the systems themselves [3].
10. **Intellectual property:** easier unauthorized copying of protected content, exposure of trade secrets, and plagiarism [3].
11. **Obscene, degrading, and/or abusive content:** easier production of such imagery, including synthetic child sexual abuse material and nonconsensual intimate images [3].
12. **Value chain and component integration:** third-party components, data or suppliers that are not transparent or properly vetted [3].

The suggested actions are organized by AI RMF subcategory, and not every subcategory is included [3]. Counted by their identifiers, there are 212 suggested actions: 58 under GOVERN, 39 under MAP, 72 under MEASURE and 43 under MANAGE [3]. They focus on four considerations: governance, content provenance, pre-deployment testing and incident disclosure [3]. Not every action applies to every user. Actions meant for developers may not fit organizations that only deploy a tool [3].

For an organization that uses generative AI in teaching, testing or client work, several risks stand out. Confabulation, data privacy, harmful bias, human-AI configuration and intellectual property are the most direct. This selection is our reading, not NIST's ranking.

## The UK Data and AI Ethics Framework

The UK's Government Digital Service first published a Data Ethics Framework in 2018 [5]. On December 18, 2025, it was retitled the Data and AI Ethics Framework to cover AI and algorithmic technologies. That update added four new principles and a self-assessment tool [5]. The 2020 version had three overarching principles: transparency, accountability and fairness [5].

The framework is written for people in government and other public sector bodies [4]. Its principles still offer a clear checklist for any organization. The seven principles, summarized in our words, are [4]:

| Principle | Summary |
|---|---|
| Transparency | Information about a project, its processes and its data is shared in a clear, accessible and free way. It includes explainability: being able to say how a system reached a decision. |
| Accountability | Projects have effective governance, oversight and routes to challenge decisions, with clear roles and responsibilities. |
| Fairness | Avoid unfair bias and impacts, remove accessibility barriers and seek positive impacts. Assess data representativeness and bias. |
| Privacy | Personal data is handled responsibly, used only for intended purposes and on an appropriate legal basis. |
| Safety | Data and systems are used without harming people, institutions or the environment. Systems are robust, secure and reliable throughout their life cycle. |
| Societal impact | Consider how the project affects wider society, including its potential for public good. |
| Environmental sustainability | Design and use data and AI to limit environmental impact, with robust and energy-efficient systems. |

The framework states that "Transparency is foundational to all other ethical principles" [4]. It also accepts that principles can conflict. Its example: more demographic data may help detect bias, but at a cost to privacy [4]. It suggests ways to handle such trade-offs. These include engaging affected communities, balancing proportionality and necessity, and monitoring outcomes [4].

Teams on AI projects are asked to use the self-assessment tool as well. The framework recommends keeping it with project records to document decisions across the life cycle [4].

## The Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard as a model public record

The Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard (ATRS) gives UK public sector bodies one format for publishing how and why they use algorithmic tools [6]. It is a requirement for all UK government departments and for certain arm's-length bodies. Other public bodies are encouraged to use it [6][7]. The template and guidance were updated in May 2025 [6].

An ATRS record has two tiers [7]:

- **Tier 1** is for the general public. It is a single summary sheet in clear, simple language [7].
- **Tier 2** is for specialist readers such as journalists, researchers and civil society. It is split across eight further sheets [7].

The Tier 2 sections cover owner and responsibility; description and rationale; deployment context; tool specification; model specification; development data; operational data; and risks, mitigations and impact assessments [7].

Several practices in the guidance transfer well to any organization [7]:

- Name the accountable owner as a role title, not a named individual.
- Give a team email address, not a personal one.
- Write in full sentences and test the draft on people outside the project.
- Explain how a person can challenge or appeal an outcome.
- Publish with brief, explained redactions rather than not publishing at all.

A private organization is not covered by the ATRS. It may still use the same structure for a voluntary public record of its own AI use.

## EU AI Act: AI in education, assessment and exam proctoring

The EU AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of June 13, 2024. It was published in the Official Journal on July 12, 2024 [8]. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of July 8, 2026, the "Digital Omnibus on AI," amended it [9]. The amending regulation was published on July 24, 2026, and entered into force on the third day after publication, July 27, 2026 [9].

**Scope.** The Act applies to providers that place AI systems on the EU market, wherever they are established. It also applies to deployers in the EU, and to providers and deployers in third countries where the system's output is used in the EU (Article 2(1)) [8]. A deployer is anyone using an AI system under its authority, other than for personal non-professional use (Article 3) [8].

**High-risk uses in education.** Article 6(2) classifies the AI systems listed in Annex III as high-risk [8]. Point 3 of Annex III covers education and vocational training. It lists AI systems intended to be used [8]:

- (a) to determine access or admission, or to assign people to educational and vocational training institutions at all levels;
- (b) to evaluate learning outcomes, including when the results steer a person's learning process;
- (c) to assess the appropriate level of education a person will receive or be able to access;
- (d) to monitor and detect prohibited behavior of students during tests. In practice this covers AI exam proctoring.

Recital 56 gives the reason. Such systems "may determine the educational and professional course of a person's life" and may affect that person's ability to secure a livelihood [8]. The 2026 amendment did not change Annex III [9].

**A narrow exception.** Under Article 6(3), an Annex III system is not high-risk where it poses no significant risk of harm, for example where it performs only a narrow procedural task [8]. This exception never applies to a system that profiles natural persons. A provider that relies on it must document its assessment (Article 6(4)) [8].

**A related prohibition.** Article 5(1)(f) prohibits AI systems that infer the emotions of a person in workplaces and education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons [8]. A proctoring tool that claims to read emotions needs particular care.

**What deployers of high-risk systems must do.** Article 26 sets out deployer duties [8]. They include using the system according to its instructions, and assigning human oversight to competent, trained people with authority. Deployers must also monitor the system and keep the logs under their control for at least six months. Where they control the input data, it must be relevant and sufficiently representative. Deployers of Annex III systems that make or assist decisions about people must inform those people (Article 26(11)) [8].

**AI literacy.** As amended in 2026, Article 4 asks providers and deployers to "take measures to support the development of AI literacy" of their staff and others operating AI on their behalf [9]. It "does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy" [9].

**Application dates.** Article 113, as amended, sets these dates [8][9]:

| Provision | Applies from |
|---|---|
| Chapters I and II, including the original prohibitions and AI literacy | February 2, 2025 |
| General date of application | August 2, 2026 |
| New prohibitions on non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material (Article 5(1)(ba) and (bb)) | December 2, 2026 |
| High-risk rules (Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3) for Annex III systems, including education and proctoring | December 2, 2027 |
| High-risk rules for Annex I systems (AI in regulated products) | August 2, 2028 |

The December 2, 2027 date replaces the original date of August 2, 2026 for Annex III systems [9]. Whether a given exam, course or credential falls within Annex III depends on the facts. That question needs advice from counsel qualified in EU law.

## A starter AI-use policy outline for a small organization

This outline links each section to the framework it draws on. The wording is ours. A small organization can fit it on two or three pages.

1. **Purpose and scope.** Say which AI tools and uses the policy covers, and who must follow it. Adopt a short set of principles, such as the UK's seven [4] or NIST's trustworthy characteristics [1].
2. **Accountability.** Name one senior role that answers for AI use (GOVERN 2.3) [1]. Name it as a role title, as the ATRS does [7].
3. **Inventory.** Keep a list of every AI use: purpose, data used, supplier, the human role and a risk rating (GOVERN 1.6) [1].
4. **Legal check.** Record the laws and contracts that apply to each use (GOVERN 1.1) [1]. For EU candidates or learners, check the use against Annex III and Article 5 of the AI Act [8].
5. **Approved and prohibited uses.** List what staff may and may not do. For example, no personal or confidential data in public generative AI tools without approval, because of the data privacy risk [3].
6. **Human oversight.** No decision about a person's admission, result or credential is made by AI alone. A trained person reviews it (GOVERN 3.2) [1]. For high-risk systems, follow Article 26(2) [8].
7. **Accuracy checks.** A person checks AI output before it is published or relied on, because of confabulation [3].
8. **Fairness and accessibility.** Test for bias and for barriers to people with disabilities before and during use [3][4].
9. **Suppliers.** Assess AI suppliers before contracting. Cover data use, intellectual property and failure plans in the contract (GOVERN 6.1, 6.2) [1][3].
10. **Transparency.** Tell people when AI is used in decisions about them [8]. Consider a public record modeled on ATRS Tier 1 [7].
11. **Training.** Give staff AI literacy training suited to their role (GOVERN 2.2) [1][9].
12. **Incidents and feedback.** Provide a way to report problems and to appeal. Log incidents and learn from them (GOVERN 4.3, 5.1) [1][3].
13. **Review and retirement.** Review the policy and the inventory on a set date. Retire tools safely when they are no longer needed (GOVERN 1.5, 1.7) [1].
