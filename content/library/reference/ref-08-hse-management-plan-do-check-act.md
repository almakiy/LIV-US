Health and safety is managed best as part of how a business is run, not as a separate system. Three free official guides show how. The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) wrote *Managing for health and safety* (HSG65) for leaders, owners, trustees and line managers [1]. HSE and the Institute of Directors issued *Leading health and safety at work* (INDG417) for boards [2]. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) published *Recommended Practices for Safety and Health Programs* for employers and workers [3]. This item explains each one, maps them to each other and ends with a monthly routine for a small business.

## The three guides at a glance

| Guide | Issuer and edition | Written for | How it is built |
|---|---|---|---|
| HSG65, Managing for health and safety [1] | HSE, third edition 2013 | Leaders, owners, trustees and line managers | Plan, Do, Check, Act; core elements; self-check questions in Part 2; key actions in Part 3 |
| INDG417(rev1), Leading health and safety at work [2] | HSE and the Institute of Directors, June 2013 | Directors, governors, trustees and officers in the private, public and third sectors, in organizations of all sizes | Three essential principles; core actions for the board under Plan, Do, Check, Act; a checklist for leaders |
| OSHA 3885, Recommended Practices [3] | OSHA, October 2016 | Employers, workers and worker representatives in any workplace outside construction | Seven core elements, each with action items |

OSHA states that its practices "are recommendations only" [3]. Employers are not cited for failing to have a program that matches them [3]. A separate OSHA document covers the construction industry [3].

## HSG65: the Plan, Do, Check, Act cycle

HSE moved from its older POPMAR model to "Plan, Do, Check, Act" [1]. HSE says the change balances the systems and behavioral sides of management. It also treats health and safety as part of good management generally [1]. HSG65 summarizes the four steps as follows [1]:

- **Plan.** Decide where you are and where you need to be. Say what you want to achieve, who is responsible and how you will measure success. Plan for emergencies and for change, and identify the legal requirements that apply.
- **Do.** Identify your risk profile and decide the priorities. Organize the work, involve workers and provide resources, including competent advice. Put controls in place, maintain equipment, train people and supervise.
- **Check.** Measure performance and make sure the plan is really in place. HSG65 warns that "'paperwork' on its own is not a good performance measure" [1]. Investigate the causes of accidents, incidents and near misses.
- **Act.** Review performance, learn from accidents, ill health and other organizations, and update plans and risk assessments. Act on lessons from audits and inspections.

The cycle is not a one-time exercise. HSG65 says you may need to go round it more than once, above all when starting out, developing something new or making a change [1].

HSG65 names the keys to managing health and safety in any organization [1]:

- leadership and management, including appropriate business processes;
- a trained and skilled workforce;
- an environment where people are trusted and involved.

These rest on an understanding of the organization's risk profile [1]. HSG65 adds that a formal management system is a choice, not a necessity. Documents should be "functional and concise" [1]. Success still depends on the attitudes and behaviors of people in the organization [1].

Part 3 of HSG65 sets out key actions under each step [1]:

| Step | Key actions in HSG65 |
|---|---|
| Plan | Determining your policy; planning for implementation |
| Do | Profiling your health and safety risks; organizing for health and safety; implementing your plan |
| Check | Measuring performance; investigating accidents and incidents |
| Act | Reviewing performance; learning lessons |

For measuring, HSG65 separates "active" monitoring, which checks controls before things go wrong, from "reactive" monitoring after the event [1]. Routine inspections are active. Accident investigations and sickness absence records are reactive [1]. It asks for leading as well as lagging indicators, not accident figures alone [1].

## Self-check questions for leaders

Part 2 of HSG65 gives leaders questions headed "What are you doing?" [1]. INDG417 includes a checklist of questions for the board, one group for each step [2]. A selection, mostly in plain words:

| Area | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Leadership | Is there visible leadership from the top? "What example do you set?" "What are your significant risks and how do you know they are being controlled?" [1] |
| Management | Who champions health and safety at board level, who sets policy and who monitors performance? "How do you control your contractors?" "How do you review your health and safety performance?" [1] |
| Competence | How are managers made aware of their duties and held accountable? Who is your competent person, and do you act on their advice? [1] |
| Worker involvement | How are employees or their representatives consulted? "Are employees consulted in good time?" [1] |
| Learning | "Have you learned from situations where things have gone wrong?" [1] |
| Plan (board) | "How do you demonstrate the board's commitment to health and safety?" [2] |
| Do (board) | How does the board make sure the organization, the board included, gets competent advice and training, and that worker concerns reach the board? [2] |
| Check (board) | What information does the board receive regularly? "Do you compare your performance with others in your sector or beyond?" [2] |
| Act (board) | "What do you do to ensure appropriate board-level review of health and safety?" [2] |

## Risk assessment scaled to the organization

HSG65 says the level of risk should decide how sophisticated the risk assessment needs to be [1]. It describes three levels [1]:

| Organization | What HSG65 expects |
|---|---|
| Small businesses with few or simple risks | A straightforward process based on informed judgment and suitable guidance |
| Medium-sized businesses or those with greater risks | A more sophisticated assessment, with specialist advice for some areas, such as a complex process or air quality measurement |
| Large and high-hazard sites | The most developed assessments. For bulk hazardous substances, large-scale mineral extraction or nuclear plant, the assessment is a significant part of the safety case or report and may use quantified risk assessment |

Whatever the size, the assessment should be done by someone who knows the activity [1]. Workers and their safety representatives are "a valuable source of information" [1]. If a consultant helps, managers and workers should still take part [1]. The assessment considers everyone who could be harmed. That includes employees, contractors, the public, users of products and services, and neighbors [1].

HSG65 also explains the UK legal test. A risk assessment must be "suitable and sufficient" under UK law [1]. In UK law, a business with fewer than five employees does not have to write the findings down [1]. Readers outside the UK should check their own national rules.

## INDG417: what the board does

INDG417 rests on three essential principles [2]:

1. **Strong and active leadership from the top:** visible, active commitment from the board; effective "downward" communication and management structures; health and safety built into business decisions [2].
2. **Worker involvement:** engaging the workforce in safe and healthy conditions; effective "upward" communication; high-quality training [2].
3. **Assessment and review:** identifying and managing risks; getting and following competent advice; monitoring, reporting and reviewing performance [2].

It then sets core actions for the board under each step [2]:

| Step | Core actions for the board in INDG417 |
|---|---|
| Plan | Know the significant risks. Agree a policy that sets out the board's own role and that of each member. "Own" the key issues and decide how to communicate and champion health and safety. Treat the policy as a living document |
| Do | Make sure arrangements are adequately resourced, competent advice is obtained, risk assessments are carried out and employees or their representatives are involved. Consider health and safety when bringing in new processes, practices or people |
| Check | Give weight to preventive information, such as training and maintenance progress, and to incident data, such as accident and sickness absence rates. Arrange periodic audits. Report the effect of major changes or failures to the board quickly. Keep up with new legal requirements |
| Act | Review health and safety performance at least once a year. Check the policy still fits, report shortcomings, decide actions and monitor them. Hold an immediate review after a major shortcoming or event |

INDG417 also suggests practical steps. Health and safety should appear regularly on the board agenda [2]. Some boards name one member as health and safety "champion" [2]. Board members can visit the "shop floor" and follow all safety measures themselves [2]. The board can also assess the arrangements of key suppliers and contractors [2]. In the UK, INDG417 notes, board members have "both collective and individual responsibility for health and safety" [2].

## OSHA: seven core elements

OSHA's practices rest on seven core elements, which it calls interrelated parts of one system [3]:

1. **Management leadership.** Top management shows commitment, sets expectations and provides resources [3].
2. **Worker participation.** Workers take part in all aspects of the program and can report concerns without fear of retaliation [3].
3. **Hazard identification and assessment.** Hazards are identified and assessed continually, and incidents are investigated to find root causes [3].
4. **Hazard prevention and control.** Employers and workers choose controls together and track them through a plan [3].
5. **Education and training.** Everyone is trained in how the program works and in recognizing hazards and controls [3].
6. **Program evaluation and improvement.** Controls and the program are evaluated, and shortcomings are corrected [3].
7. **Communication and coordination for host employers, contractors and staffing agencies.** All parties at a worksite give the same level of protection and coordinate their work [3].

OSHA lists controls in order of preference. It starts with "engineering solutions first, followed by safe work practices, administrative controls, and finally personal protective equipment (PPE)" [3]. It advises evaluating the program initially and at least annually [3]. It also suggests both kinds of indicator [3]. Lagging indicators track injuries and illnesses that have already occurred. Leading indicators include hazards and near misses reported, response times, management walkthroughs and training completed [3].

OSHA says the practices suit small, medium-sized and large organizations [3]. Small employers may find informal communications and procedures work best [3]. For a first step, it offers "10 easy things" to get a program started [3]. They include leading by example, setting up a reporting system, inspecting the workplace with workers and planning for emergencies [3].

## Mapping the three guides

| Theme | HSG65 [1] | INDG417 [2] | OSHA [3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leadership and policy | Plan: determining your policy; leadership as a core element | Principle 1; Plan core actions | Management leadership |
| Worker involvement | Do: involve workers and communicate; worker involvement as a core element | Principle 2; Do: employees involved in decisions | Worker participation |
| Finding and ranking risks | Do: profiling your health and safety risks | Principle 3; Do: risk assessments carried out | Hazard identification and assessment |
| Putting controls in place | Do: implementing your plan (controls, equipment, supervision) | Do: adequate resources; health and safety designed into change | Hazard prevention and control |
| Competence and training | Competence as a core element; Do: train and instruct; access to competent advice | Principle 2 (training) and Principle 3 (competent advice); Do core actions | Education and training |
| Contractors and shared workplaces | Plan: co-operate with others who share your workplace; "How do you control your contractors?" | Do: assess partners, key suppliers and contractors; Check: reports on contractors | Communication and coordination for host employers, contractors and staffing agencies |
| Emergencies | Plan: consider fire and other emergencies | Not addressed separately | Hazard identification covers emergency and nonroutine situations |
| Measuring and investigating | Check: measuring performance; investigating accidents, incidents and near misses | Check core actions | Program evaluation (indicators); incident investigation under hazard identification |
| Review and learning | Act: reviewing performance; learning lessons | Act: board review at least once a year | Program evaluation and improvement |

The three guides differ in focus more than in substance. HSG65 is the fullest account of the management cycle [1]. INDG417 is a short leaflet that speaks to the board [2]. OSHA gives numbered action items for each element [3].

## A monthly routine for a small business owner

HSG65 notes that in a small business the owner's own behavior "determines the outcome" [1]. A formal, documented system is not always necessary [1]. The routine below is one way to apply the three guides each month. Adjust it to your risks.

1. **Walk the workplace with a worker.** Ask what activity, equipment or material concerns them [3]. Check that controls are really used, not only written down [1].
2. **Look at the reports.** Review injuries, near misses, hazards and concerns raised, and how fast you responded [3]. Close or chase every open action [1][3].
3. **Hold a short safety talk.** Smaller businesses often use face-to-face discussion and toolbox talks [1]. Ask for ideas and follow them up [3].
4. **Check what has changed.** New work, equipment, materials, people or contractors may mean the risk assessment needs updating [1]. Consult workers before significant changes [3].
5. **Check training and advice.** Confirm that new and moved staff are trained for their tasks and that refresher training happens on time [1]. Note any point where you need competent advice [1][2].
6. **Write down three or four indicators.** Mix leading measures, such as inspections done and actions closed, with lagging ones, such as injuries [1][3].

Once a year, step back and review the whole approach: policy, risks, results and lessons [2][3]. Review sooner after a serious incident or a major change [2].
