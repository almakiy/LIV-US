Each year, the head of a federal executive agency states whether the agency's internal control works. OMB Circular A-123, revised March 10, 2026, sets out how agencies assess internal control and report on it [1]. Its Attachment 3 gives three illustrative assurance statements [1].

A private owner or board can borrow the same discipline: assess, conclude, sign and fix. This template adapts that pattern for a company, family business or nonprofit. It is a voluntary management tool, not a federal filing and not an audit opinion.

## What A-123 asks of a federal agency

A-123 requires agencies to assess and report on internal control annually [1]. Management gives its assurances, with information on material weaknesses and corrective action, in a single report. That report is the Agency Financial Report, the Performance and Accountability Report or another management report [1]. The circular calls the assurance statement "an accountability statement so only essential information must be included" [1]. Agencies keep enough documentation internally to support it [1].

A-123 describes the assessment as a sequence of steps [1]:
- evaluate internal control for each principle and each objective;
- keep a summary log of all identified deficiencies;
- conclude, principle by principle, whether each is designed, implemented and operating effectively;
- conclude the same for each of the five components;
- conclude on the system as a whole, including whether the components operate together in an integrated manner; and
- judge how severe the deficiencies are, alone and in combination across the components.

The five components and 17 principles are those of GAO's *Standards for Internal Control in the Federal Government*, the Green Book [2]. A-123 lists them in its Table 1 [1]. A companion item in this collection explains them.

### Three levels of deficiency

A-123 sorts control problems into three levels in its Table 2 [1]:
- **Control deficiency:** the design, implementation or operation of a control does not let management or staff, in the normal course of their work, achieve the entity's objectives. It is reported inside the organization.
- **Significant deficiency:** less severe than a material weakness, yet important enough to merit attention by those charged with governance. It is also reported inside the organization.
- **Material weakness:** a deficiency, or a combination of them, that the agency head judges significant or severe enough to report outside the agency. Failing to achieve a relevant principle and its component results in a material weakness.

If one or more components are not operating effectively, A-123 says a material weakness must be reported [1]. The Green Book defines a deficiency in almost the same words (OV3.07) [2].

## The three kinds of assurance

Attachment 3 of A-123 shows three forms of statement through examples, not through a decision rule [1]. In plain words:
- **Unmodified assurance.** Management can provide reasonable assurance that internal control over operations, reporting and compliance was operating effectively as of the year-end date. The statement lists no exceptions.
- **Modified assurance.** Management can provide the same reasonable assurance, except for material weaknesses that it names and briefly describes.
- **No assurance.** Management is unable to provide assurance that internal control was operating effectively, because of the material weaknesses that it names.

All three examples open the same way [1]. Management states that it is responsible for managing risks and maintaining effective internal control, and that it assessed risk and internal control under the circular. Each example then states, separately, whether the accounting system conforms to the applicable principles, standards and requirements. The agency head signs.

For a private organization, the editors suggest a simple reading. Choose unmodified when no material weakness remains at year-end. Choose modified when material weaknesses exist but control works outside them. Choose no assurance when weaknesses are pervasive or the assessment was too incomplete to support a conclusion. This reading is a suggestion, not OMB text.

## Adapting the pattern to a private organization

Four changes make the A-123 pattern fit a private organization [1][2]:
- **The signer** is the owner, or the chair on behalf of the board, instead of an agency head.
- **The criteria are named accurately.** An organization may say it assessed internal control with reference to the Green Book. If it says it adopted the Green Book as criteria, it follows all relevant requirements (Green Book OV4.20).
- **The date** is the organization's own year-end. The federal examples use September 30.
- **The readers** are the owners or the board, and lenders, funders or members only if the organization chooses. An entity outside the federal executive branch may look to applicable laws and to input from key external stakeholders when deciding how to report (Green Book OV3.01).

Do not describe the statement as approved by GAO or OMB. Name the documents only as the reference used.

## Template 1: Annual assurance statement

Copy the text below. Replace each item in square brackets, and delete the conclusion options that do not apply.

> **[Organization name]**
>
> **Statement on governance and internal control for the year ended [date]**
>
> **1. Responsibility.** The [owner / board of directors] of [organization name] is responsible for managing risks and for maintaining effective internal control over operations, reporting and compliance.
>
> **2. Basis of this statement.** [Management / the owner] assessed risk and internal control for the year ended [date]. The assessment was made with reference to the five components and 17 principles in the U.S. Government Accountability Office's *Standards for Internal Control in the Federal Government* (GAO-25-107721) and the assessment steps in OMB Circular No. A-123. It drew on: [risk register; principle-by-principle evaluation; deficiency log; results of monitoring and spot checks; reports from the outside accountant or auditor; other sources].
>
> **3. Conclusion.** [Keep one option and delete the others.]
>
> *Option A, unmodified.* Based on the results of the assessment, [organization name] can provide reasonable assurance that internal control over operations, reporting and compliance was operating effectively as of [date].
>
> *Option B, modified.* Based on the results of the assessment, [organization name] can provide reasonable assurance that internal control over operations, reporting and compliance was operating effectively as of [date], except for the following material weaknesses: [brief description of each material weakness]. The attached corrective action plan sets out how and when each will be fixed.
>
> *Option C, no assurance.* Based on the results of the assessment, [organization name] is unable to provide assurance that internal control over operations, reporting and compliance was operating effectively as of [date], due to the following material weaknesses: [brief description of each material weakness]. The attached corrective action plan sets out how and when each will be fixed.
>
> **4. Financial records (optional).** The accounting records of [organization name] are kept in accordance with [the accounting framework used] [, except as follows: brief description].
>
> **5. Limits.** Internal control gives reasonable, not absolute, assurance. Error, collusion, management override and events outside our control can still cause failures.
>
> **6. Follow-up.** Progress on open corrective actions will be reported to the [owners / board] every [period] until each action is complete and validated.
>
> Signed: ____________________ [Name, role]  Date: [date]
>
> Approved at [the board meeting / the owners' meeting] held on [date], minute reference [reference].

## Template 3: Corrective action plan

A-123 expects organizations to document the steps taken to resolve each issue, the timeline and the responsible parties [1]. A plan may include the root cause analysis, milestones, planned actions, measurable indicators of remediation, the responsible official and the completion date [1]. The Green Book lists similar contents for corrective action records (paragraph 17.06) [2].

Rules for using the plan, drawn from A-123 [1]:
- Start corrective action without delay, to limit harm to operations.
- Find the root cause of each deficiency. Auditors are not responsible for identifying root causes, so do not rely only on their recommendations.
- Give the owner or board timely updates, and regular progress reports when an action runs late.
- Close an item only after the actions are taken and validated. Record that decision in writing, with the supporting evidence.
- Keep the detailed plan on file to support the statement.

## Before signing: a short checklist

These questions come from the A-123 assessment steps and the Green Book's documentation requirements [1][2]:
- Has every principle been evaluated, with the evidence on file?
- Is every identified deficiency in the log, with its level?
- Does the conclusion match the log, with no material weakness left out?
- Does each material weakness have a corrective action, a responsible person and a date?
- Has someone who does not run the area reviewed the results? Separate evaluations give greater objectivity when the reviewer is not responsible for the activity (Green Book 16.07).
