The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) publishes the *Standards for Internal Control in the Federal Government*, known as the Green Book. The current edition is GAO-25-107721, dated May 2025 [1]. It supersedes the September 2014 edition (GAO-14-704G). It is effective beginning with fiscal year 2026 and the FMFIA reports covering that year, and early adoption is permitted [1].

The Green Book is written for federal agencies. Its method, however, suits any organization that wants a clear and tested structure for internal control. This item explains how the standards are built and how a smaller organization can use them.

## Why the Green Book exists and whom it binds

The Federal Managers' Financial Integrity Act of 1982 (FMFIA), at 31 U.S.C. 3512(c) and (d), requires the Comptroller General to issue internal control standards. It also requires federal executive branch entities to establish internal control in accordance with them [1]. OMB Circular A-123 provides the requirements and guidance for how executive agencies evaluate and report on internal control (paragraph OV3.01) [1]. The 2026 revision of A-123 lists the same five components and 17 principles in its Table 1 [2].

Other entities may adopt the Green Book. GAO names federal entities outside the executive branch and nonfederal entities, such as state, local and quasi-governmental entities and nonprofit organizations (OV4.20) [1]. Management decides, based on the laws and regulations that apply to it, how to adapt the standards. If management elects to adopt the Green Book as criteria, it follows all relevant requirements [1].

In the Green Book's definition, internal control is "a process effected by an entity's oversight body, management, and other personnel" (OV1.01) [1]. The process is "designed to provide reasonable assurance that the objectives of an entity will be achieved" [1]. Objectives fall into three categories: operations, reporting and compliance. Reasonable assurance is not absolute assurance. Human error, collusion, management override and events outside the entity's control can defeat any system (OV1.08) [1].

## How the standards are built

The Green Book uses a hierarchy of five components and 17 principles (OV2.03) [1]. Below the principles sit attributes. Attributes explain a principle more precisely and may give examples of suitable procedures. Apart from the minimum documentation requirements they contain, attributes do not impose requirements (OV2.08) [1].

GAO's letter states that COSO's *Internal Control - Integrated Framework* introduced the concept of principles related to the five components. It adds that the Green Book adapts these principles for a government environment [1].

### "Must" and "should"

The Green Book signals its requirements with two verbs, defined in its glossary [1]:
- **Must** applies to the five components. It denotes "a requirement that management must comply with in all cases." All five components must be effectively designed, implemented and operating, and must operate together in an integrated manner (OV2.04).
- **Should** applies to the 17 principles. It denotes a principle requirement that management should comply with "except in rare circumstances where the requirement is not relevant for the entity."

So "should" in the Green Book is still a requirement, not a mere suggestion. If management decides that a principle is not relevant, it documents the rationale for how the component can still work without it (OV2.06) [1].

Management makes a summary determination for each principle. If a principle is not designed, implemented or operating effectively, its component cannot be effective. If any component fails, or the components do not operate together, the internal control system is ineffective (OV3.10, OV3.11) [1].

## The five components and 17 principles in plain words

The table restates each principle in plain words. The official wording is in Appendix I of the Green Book [1], and the component descriptions follow paragraph OV2.04 [1].

| Component | No. | The principle in plain words |
|---|---|---|
| **Control environment**: the foundation, giving discipline and structure | 1 | The oversight body and management show, by word and example, that integrity and ethical values come first. |
| | 2 | The oversight body watches over the internal control system. |
| | 3 | Management sets up a structure, assigns responsibilities and delegates authority so that objectives can be met. |
| | 4 | Management recruits, develops and keeps competent people. |
| | 5 | Management evaluates performance and holds people to account for their internal control duties. |
| **Risk assessment**: identifying and analyzing risks to objectives | 6 | Management states objectives clearly enough to spot risks, and defines how much variation it will tolerate. |
| | 7 | Management identifies, analyzes and responds to risks to those objectives. |
| | 8 | Management looks specifically at fraud, improper payments and information security risks. |
| | 9 | Management identifies, analyzes and responds to significant changes that could affect internal control. |
| **Control activities**: actions set through policies and procedures | 10 | Management designs control activities that bring risks down to acceptable levels. |
| | 11 | Management designs general control activities over information technology for the same purpose. |
| | 12 | Management puts control activities in place through policies and procedures. |
| **Information and communication**: quality information that supports control | 13 | Management obtains or produces relevant, quality information and uses it to run internal control. |
| | 14 | Management shares that information inside the entity, including objectives and control responsibilities. |
| | 15 | Management shares relevant information with the right outside parties. |
| **Monitoring**: checking performance over time and resolving findings | 16 | Management sets up and runs monitoring activities and evaluates the results. |
| | 17 | Management fixes identified internal control deficiencies on a timely basis. |

Under principle 7, the Green Book names four kinds of risk response: acceptance, avoidance, reduction and sharing, such as insuring against losses (7.10) [1]. Under principle 8, it names three types of fraud: fraudulent reporting, misappropriation of assets and other illegal acts (8.06) [1].

## Documentation requirements

Documentation is "a necessary part of an effective internal control system" (OV2.10) [1]. Its level and nature may vary with the entity's size and the complexity of its processes. Management uses judgment on how much is needed. Management also develops and maintains documentation of its internal control system (3.09) [1].

On top of that general duty, Appendix I lists seven minimum documentation requirements [1]. The text marks each one with the words "[documentation requirement]".

| Paragraph | What management documents |
|---|---|
| OV2.06 | Why a principle is not relevant, and how the component still works without it |
| 7.15 | The results of periodic and ongoing risk assessments, including how fraud, improper payments, information security and significant changes were considered |
| 9.05 | A change assessment process, set up in advance, so that control can adapt quickly to significant changes |
| 12.02 | Policies that say what is expected, and procedures that set out the actions that carry the policies out |
| 16.09 | The evaluated results of ongoing monitoring and separate evaluations |
| 17.05 | The evaluation of internal control issues and the corrective actions chosen, including for audit findings |
| 17.06 | The completion of corrective actions, including for audit findings |

These seven are the minimum. A gap in meeting them counts in management's summary determination for the related principle (OV2.12) [1]. Records of corrective actions may include root cause analysis, planned actions, interim milestones, completion dates, measurable indicators and the official responsible for monitoring status (17.06) [1].

## What changed from the 2014 edition

The 2014 edition took effect with fiscal year 2016 [3]. GAO's letter for the 2025 revision lists these key changes [1]:
- risks related to improper payments and information security are now considered alongside fraud when identifying, analyzing and responding to risks;
- the results of risk assessments are documented;
- a change assessment process is documented, so that control can adapt quickly to significant changes; and
- two new appendixes give examples of control activities and sources of data, and point to further resources on fraud, improper payments and information security.

The letter also notes an emphasis on prioritizing preventive control activities. It highlights management's responsibility for internal control at all levels, such as program and financial managers [1]. Preventive controls generally offer the most cost-efficient use of resources and help avoid the costly "pay and chase model" (OV4.16) [1].

A side-by-side reading of the two Appendix I lists shows the effect [1][3]:

| Item | 2014 edition | 2025 edition |
|---|---|---|
| Principle 8 | Consider the potential for fraud | Consider risks related to fraud, improper payments and information security |
| Principle 10 | Design control activities to achieve objectives and respond to risks | Design control activities to mitigate risks to acceptable levels |
| Principle 11 | Design the entity's information system and related control activities | Design general control activities over information technology |
| Principle 12 | Implement control activities through policies | Implement control activities through policies and procedures |
| Paragraph 12.02 | Document in policies the internal control responsibilities | Document policies (what is expected) and procedures (the actions) |
| Paragraphs 17.05 and 17.06 | Internal control deficiencies | Expressly include deficiencies reported from internal and external audits and evaluations |
| Minimum documentation list | Six items, including paragraph 3.09 | Seven items, adding 7.15 and 9.05; paragraph 3.09 is stated before the list as a general duty |
| Effective | Fiscal year 2016 | Fiscal year 2026 |

## How small entities apply it

The 17 principles apply to both large and small entities, but smaller entities may use different approaches (OV4.13) [1]. Small entities often have advantages: managers are closely involved in processes and deal directly with staff. Informal staff meetings may be enough to share quality information, where larger entities need written reports or formal meetings [1].

The main difficulty for a small entity is segregation of duties, because responsibilities are concentrated in a few people (OV4.14) [1]. GAO suggests compensating designs. Examples are extra levels of review for key processes, review of randomly selected transactions and their support, periodic asset counts and checks of supervisor reconciliations [1]. Where segregation is not practical, management designs alternative control activities (10.23) [1].

Three more points keep the effort proportionate [1]:
- Documentation may be lighter in a smaller, simpler entity, but the minimum documentation requirements still apply (OV2.10, OV2.12).
- The oversight body may be one or a few members of senior management (OV2.19). When managers also form the oversight body, their oversight role is kept distinct from their management role (2.02, 2.03).
- Costs and benefits are weighed, but "cost alone is not an acceptable reason to avoid implementing internal controls" (OV4.17).

## Voluntary use by any organization

The Green Book binds federal executive branch entities through FMFIA. For other entities it is a framework they may choose to use (OV2.01, OV4.20) [1]. A private company, a shop or a family firm can borrow its structure as a reference model.

Two cautions apply. First, an entity that says it has adopted the Green Book as criteria takes on all relevant requirements (OV4.20) [1]. Second, the federal reporting rules apply to executive agencies, which A-123 defines by reference to 31 U.S.C. 102 [2]. An entity outside the federal executive branch may look to applicable laws and to input from key external stakeholders when deciding how to evaluate and report on its internal control (OV3.01) [1].

A practical sequence for a small business, built from the principles and documentation requirements above [1]:
1. Write down the few objectives that matter most for operations, reporting and compliance (principle 6).
2. List the main risks, including fraud and information security, and record the response to each: accept, avoid, reduce or share (7.10, 7.15).
3. Decide in advance how a big change, such as a new system or the loss of a key person, will trigger a fresh look at risks (9.05).
4. Put the key controls into short written policies and procedures (12.02).
5. Check that they work during the year, and record what you find (16.09).
6. Fix what you find, with a named person and a date, and record the result (17.05, 17.06).
7. Once a year, state in writing how well internal control worked. A companion template in this collection gives a form for that statement.

## Minimum practice for a small business, by component

The right-hand column gives suggested minimum practices for proportionate use. They are not GAO text. They draw on the Green Book's examples for small entities (OV4.14) [1]. They also draw on its common categories of control activities, such as authorization of transactions, physical control over vulnerable assets and segregation of duties (Table 1) [1].

| Component | What the Green Book asks for | Suggested minimum practice for a small business |
|---|---|---|
| Control environment | Integrity and ethics, oversight, structure, competence and accountability (principles 1 to 5) | A one-page code of conduct that everyone signs; written authority limits; the owners review the business at least quarterly in a meeting with minutes |
| Risk assessment | Clear objectives and risk tolerances; risks including fraud, improper payments and information security; significant change (principles 6 to 9) | A one-page risk register with the chosen response to each risk, reviewed at least twice a year and after any big change |
| Control activities | Control activities that bring risk to acceptable levels, general controls over technology, policies and procedures (principles 10 to 12) | Two-person approval of payments above a set amount; daily cash counts; a monthly bank reconciliation reviewed by the owner; personal logins with limited access rights; regular backups |
| Information and communication | Quality information, shared inside and outside the entity (principles 13 to 15) | The same short monthly report every month; brief team meetings; a known way to raise concerns directly with the owners |
| Monitoring | Monitoring activities and timely remediation (principles 16 and 17) | The owner's spot checks of randomly chosen transactions; a yearly review by an outside accountant; a corrective action log with a person and a date for each item |
