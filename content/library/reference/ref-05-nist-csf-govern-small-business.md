Cybersecurity is a governance question, not only a technical one. Version 2.0 of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF), published on February 26, 2024, makes this explicit. It added a GOVERN function and placed it at the center of the framework [1].

This guide explains what GOVERN asks of owners and boards. It also covers profiles and tiers, and what NIST's Small Business Quick-Start Guide suggests [1][2]. It ends with a one-page starter checklist for a shop or small firm.

## What the CSF is, and what it is not

The CSF describes outcomes. It "does not prescribe how outcomes should be achieved" [1]. NIST also states that the outcomes "are not a checklist of actions to perform" [1]. Each organization chooses its own actions to reach them.

It is meant for any organization. NIST designed it for "organizations of all sizes and sectors, including industry, government, academia, and nonprofit organizations" [1]. NIST describes its own role this way: "NIST is not a regulatory agency, and most organizations use the CSF on a voluntary basis" [3].

The CSF is a tool to use, not a badge to earn. NIST "does not offer certifications or endorsements of CSF-related products, implementations, or services" [3].

## Six functions, with GOVERN at the center

The CSF Core is a hierarchy of Functions, Categories and Subcategories [1]. Counted from Appendix A of CSF 2.0, it has 6 Functions, 22 Categories and 106 Subcategories [1]. NIST names each Function after a verb and gives it a one-line outcome [1]:

| Function | NIST's outcome statement |
|---|---|
| GOVERN (GV) | "The organization's cybersecurity risk management strategy, expectations, and policy are established, communicated, and monitored." |
| IDENTIFY (ID) | "The organization's current cybersecurity risks are understood." |
| PROTECT (PR) | "Safeguards to manage the organization's cybersecurity risks are used." |
| DETECT (DE) | "Possible cybersecurity attacks and compromises are found and analyzed." |
| RESPOND (RS) | "Actions regarding a detected cybersecurity incident are taken." |
| RECOVER (RC) | "Assets and operations affected by a cybersecurity incident are restored." |

NIST draws the Functions as a wheel. "GOVERN is in the center of the wheel because it informs how an organization will implement the other five Functions" [1]. The Functions "should be addressed concurrently" [1]. Their order and size in the Core do not imply sequence or importance [1].

GOVERN is new in version 2.0. NIST explains that governance activities used to sit inside the Identify Function, which limited their visibility [3].

## The six GOVERN categories in plain words

GOVERN has six Categories and 31 Subcategories [1]. The table restates each Category in plain words. The counts come from Appendix A [1].

| Category | What it means in practice | Subcategories |
|---|---|---|
| Organizational Context (GV.OC) | Know your mission and your stakeholders. Know what others rely on you for and what you rely on. Know your legal, regulatory and contractual cybersecurity duties, including privacy. | 5 |
| Risk Management Strategy (GV.RM) | Agree risk objectives. Write down risk appetite and tolerance. Fold cyber risk into overall risk management. Use one method to rate risks, and consider opportunities as well as threats. | 7 |
| Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorities (GV.RR) | Leadership is accountable. Say who does what. Give the work enough resources. Include cybersecurity in human resources practices. | 4 |
| Policy (GV.PO) | Set a cybersecurity policy that fits your context and priorities. Communicate and enforce it. Review it when requirements, threats, technology or mission change. | 2 |
| Oversight (GV.OV) | Review the results of the strategy and the organization's performance. Adjust the strategy when needed. | 3 |
| Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management (GV.SC) | Know your suppliers and rank them by criticality. Check them before you sign. Put security terms in contracts. Monitor them, include them in incident plans, and plan for the end of each relationship. | 10 |

Two outcomes speak directly to leaders. GV.RR-01 reads: "Organizational leadership is responsible and accountable for cybersecurity risk and fosters a culture that is risk-aware, ethical, and continually improving" [1]. GV.RM-02 asks that risk appetite and risk tolerance statements are "established, communicated, and maintained" [1].

NIST also describes the executive role. Executives "communicate expectations about risk appetite, accountability, and resources" [1]. They are "also responsible for integrating cybersecurity risk management with ERM programs" [1]. ERM means enterprise risk management. Small organizations, NIST notes, may by their nature monitor risk at the enterprise level [1].

## Profiles: where you are and where you want to be

An Organizational Profile describes an organization's "current and/or target cybersecurity posture" in terms of the Core's outcomes [1]. It has one or both of two parts [1]:

- A **Current Profile** lists the outcomes the organization is achieving now, and how well.
- A **Target Profile** lists the outcomes it has chosen and prioritized for its risk objectives.

A Community Profile is a published baseline for a sector, technology or threat. An organization can use one as the basis for its Target Profile [1].

NIST outlines five steps [1]:

1. Scope the profile, for example the whole firm or only its financial systems [1].
2. Gather the information needed, such as policies, risk priorities and requirements.
3. Create the profile.
4. Analyze the gaps between Current and Target, and write a prioritized action plan.
5. Carry out the plan and update the profile [1].

The steps can be repeated as often as needed [1]. A Current Profile can also show customers or partners what the organization already does. A Target Profile can tell suppliers what is expected of them [1].

## Tiers: rigor, not a grade

The CSF has four Tiers: Partial (Tier 1), Risk Informed (Tier 2), Repeatable (Tier 3) and Adaptive (Tier 4) [1]. Tiers "characterize the rigor of an organization's cybersecurity risk governance and management practices" [1]. They describe a progression from informal, ad hoc responses to approaches that are agile and continuously improving [1].

| Tier | Governance side, as NIST's notional table describes it |
|---|---|
| 1 Partial | The risk strategy is applied ad hoc. Priorities are not formally based on objectives or the threat environment. |
| 2 Risk Informed | Management approves risk practices, but they may not be organization-wide policy. |
| 3 Repeatable | Risk practices are formally approved and expressed as policy, and they are regularly updated. |
| 4 Adaptive | An organization-wide approach. Executives monitor cybersecurity risk alongside financial and other risks, and the budget reflects the risk environment. |

Three points in the CSF matter here. Tiers are optional: "An organization can choose to use the Tiers" [1]. They "should complement an organization's cybersecurity risk management methodology rather than replace it" [1]. And moving up is encouraged "when risks or mandates are greater or when a cost-benefit analysis indicates a feasible and cost-effective reduction of negative cybersecurity risks" [1].

Read together, these points mean a Tier is not a maturity score to push as high as possible. The right Tier is the one that fits the organization's risks and resources. The quick-start guide adds that Tiers can be set by Function or by Category [2].

## What the Small Business Quick-Start Guide suggests

NIST Special Publication 1300 was published in February 2024 [2]. It is written for small and medium businesses, "specifically those who have modest or no cybersecurity plans in place" [2]. It can also help nonprofits, government agencies and schools. It is a supplement to the CSF and does not replace it [2].

The guide suggests GOVERN actions under four verbs [2]:

- **Understand** how cybersecurity risks can disrupt your mission (GV.OC-01). Understand your legal, regulatory and contractual requirements (GV.OC-03). Understand who will develop and carry out the strategy (GV.RR-02) [2].
- **Assess** the impact of a total or partial loss of critical assets and operations (GV.OC-04). Assess whether cybersecurity insurance is appropriate (GV.RM-04). Assess supplier risks before entering formal relationships (GV.SC-06) [2].
- **Prioritize** managing cybersecurity risks alongside other business risks (GV.RM-03) [2].
- **Communicate** leadership's support for a risk-aware, ethical and continually improving culture (GV.RR-01). Communicate, enforce and maintain policies (GV.PO-01) [2].

It offers simple worksheets. One asks for the mission statement and the cyber risks that could prevent it. Another asks you to list legal, regulatory and contractual requirements [2]. It also poses questions [2]:

- As the business grows, how often is the cybersecurity strategy reviewed?
- Should the business upskill staff, hire, or engage an outside partner?
- Are there acceptable use policies for business and employee-owned devices, and do staff know them?

If an owner does not feel able to handle an item alone, the guide can serve as a discussion prompt with a provider. One example it gives is a managed security service provider [2].

Outside GOVERN, the guide calls multi-factor authentication "one of the fastest, cheapest ways you can protect your data" [2]. It also suggests changing default passwords, patching with automatic updates, and backing up data and testing the backups. It recommends a basic incident response plan with a list of whom to call [2].

## A one-page starter checklist, by GOVERN category

This checklist is a starting point built from the GOVERN outcomes [1] and the quick-start guide [2]. The wording and examples are ours, not NIST's. Adapt it to your own risks.

**Organizational context (GV.OC)**

- Write the business mission in one or two sentences. List the cyber risks that could stop it (GV.OC-01) [2].
- List your legal, regulatory and contractual cybersecurity requirements, including privacy duties (GV.OC-03) [1][2].
- List what customers rely on you for, and what you rely on: payments, internet, cloud services, your accountant (GV.OC-04, GV.OC-05) [1].

**Risk management strategy (GV.RM)**

- State in plain words how much risk you accept. An example: "we can be offline for a day, but not a week" (GV.RM-02) [1].
- Put cyber risks on the same risk list as your other business risks (GV.RM-03) [2].
- Decide whether cybersecurity insurance is appropriate (GV.RM-04) [2].
- Rate every risk the same way, for example by likelihood and impact (GV.RM-06) [1].

**Roles, responsibilities and authorities (GV.RR)**

- The owner or board accepts accountability and tells staff so (GV.RR-01) [1][2].
- Name one person responsible for the plan, and any outside provider who helps (GV.RR-02) [2].
- Set aside a budget and time for the work (GV.RR-03) [1].
- Build cybersecurity into hiring, training and departures, including removing access promptly (GV.RR-04) [1][2].

**Policy (GV.PO)**

- Keep a short written policy, including acceptable use of business and personal devices (GV.PO-01) [2].
- Make sure staff have read it. Review it on a set date and whenever threats, technology or the business change (GV.PO-02) [1].

**Oversight (GV.OV)**

- Review a short report on a fixed schedule: incidents, near misses and open actions (GV.OV-01, GV.OV-03) [1].
- Adjust the plan as the business grows [2].

**Cybersecurity supply chain (GV.SC)**

- List the suppliers that touch your data or systems, ranked by criticality (GV.SC-04) [1].
- Check a supplier's security before you sign (GV.SC-06) [2].
- Put security terms in contracts and agreements (GV.SC-05) [1].
- Include key suppliers in your incident response contact list (GV.SC-08) [1][2].
- Plan for the end of each contract: return or deletion of data, and removal of access (GV.SC-10) [1].
